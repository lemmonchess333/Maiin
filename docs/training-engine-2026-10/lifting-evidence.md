# Lifting programming for Tropos: the evidence base, expected progress, and the Lift4 gap analysis

Prepared 2026-10-06 by a research subagent for the multi-agent run that will build
the lifting model (powerlifting/strength, hypertrophy, powerbuilding, general
fitness, lifters who run) and a simulation of synthetic lifters over 16–52
weeks. It is planning material: nothing in it is decided. Index, verification
status and corrections: [README.md](README.md).

**Verification pass (2026-10-07).** All 121 sources then in the source table were checked against PubMed, Europe PMC full text, Crossref, the publisher or the book itself, and a second agent re-checked every disputed claim: each one the first check corrected or found unsupported, and each load-bearing one it could not check. The 16 rows added to the table since come from the deep reads and the gap check that followed, and carry their own markers.
Across all claims, 390 were confirmed, 115 corrected and 47 unsupported, and 39 could not be checked. The main corrections:

- Pelland: counting an indirect set as 0.5 fitted best for strength as well as size; direct-only counting fitted weekly strength worst.
- Pelland: past about 3–4 fractional sets a week, extra sets stop adding detectable strength; the threshold is not about 5 direct sets.
- Grgic 2018: with volume equated, training frequency made no clear difference to strength (p = 0.421).
- Grgic 2022: the authors find failure does not appear to cost strength, and put the non-failure groups' edge down to their doing more sets.
- Coleman 2024: a week off lost no strength; both groups gained, the week-off group a little less in the lower body, by an uncertain margin.
- Tapers: Bosquet 2007 supports about 2 weeks, not 2–3; Travis 2020 says cut volume 30–70% over 1–2 weeks, then take 2–7 days off, not "about half over 2 ± 1 weeks".
- Nuzzo 2024: its mean reps at each load replace the textbook table (9.8 at 80%, 8.8 on the bench), and a rep is worth about 2–3% of 1RM, not 2.5–3.5%.
- Latella 2020 gives one rate per lifter over 1.6–1.8 years of follow-up, not a 15-year curve; the ACSM 40/20/16/10/2% gains are from the 2002 stand, not 2009.

These 10 load-bearing claims are still marked [U] (unchecked) or [R] (source not identified), and a simulator parameter, a default or the app's copy would use them. Do not encode them:

- Helms 2015 on keeping load, effort and volume through a cut (L4, L30; §7 rule 18) [U].
- 5/3/1's ~10% reset, cited for the two-miss drop (L19) [U].
- LeSuer 1997's 9–14% deadlift under-prediction, for an e1RM correction [U].
- Bosquet 2013's detraining rates, and little loss in the first 2–3 weeks off [U].
- The trial in trained men that found no 1RM loss after two weeks off (§2.18) [R].
- A flat 30% shrink of the observed spread, credited to Dankel & Loenneke [U].
- Fatigue time constants of 13–22 days for Busso 1990 and 1994, seen only in Peng 2023's collection [U].
- Which muscles Ahtiainen 2016's size measure covers, for the novice leg-size anchor (§4.3) [U].
- The McDonald and Aragon lean-mass convention (§4.3), a coaching convention with no research source [R].
- Women's relative upper-body strength being lower than their lower-body strength, for L14's starting-load factor [R].

## Read this first

**How the evidence was gathered.** The first draft was written on 2026-10-06,
while the egress proxy blocked WebFetch and shell access to PubMed, PMC, Europe
PMC, Crossref, SportRxiv, every publisher site tried, Stronger By Science and
Wikipedia. It rested on abstract and summary text from web search for about 50
sources, and on prior knowledge for the rest. On 2026-10-07 the sources
themselves were read: the full text where it was open (Europe PMC and PMC,
SportRxiv and arXiv preprints, OSF files, author and university repositories),
and the PubMed abstract, Crossref record, publisher page or book where it was
not. The apps in §5 were read on their own help centres, blogs, product pages
and App Store listings. A few publisher sites stayed blocked, so a closed paper
was checked against its abstract and, where one exists, its preprint; the
published Pelland paper is one. Each claim now carries one of these markers:

| Marker | Meaning                                                                                                                    |
| ------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [VF]   | Checked against the full text on 2026-10-07.                                                                               |
| [VA]   | Checked against the abstract, or the book or web page itself, on 2026-10-07.                                               |
| [U]    | The source was identified, but this claim could not be checked: the paper is paywalled, or the abstract does not state it. |
| [R]    | Still not identified or not checked. Do not encode it.                                                                     |
| [C]    | Computed by script from published numbers, not quoted from a source.                                                       |

These replace the first draft's markers, which recorded search snippets, a
secondary source or the sibling running agent, and recall. [R] no longer means
"already in the repo".

**Grades** (on claims): **STRONG** means meta-analyses or consistent RCTs.
**MODERATE** means several trials or one good meta-analysis with caveats.
**WEAK** means a single study, small samples, indirect evidence or expert
consensus. **CONVENTION** means coaching practice with no trial behind it.

**Population caveat.** Most trials run 6–12 weeks on young men who are untrained
or "recreationally trained". Advanced lifters, women, older adults and
anything longer than six months are underrepresented. Every projection beyond
12 weeks below is an extrapolation and says so.

**Not redone (already in the repo):** Zatsiorsky, Kraemer & Fry; Fleck & Kraemer;
Helms et al.'s _Pyramid_; Israetel et al.; Schoenfeld's textbook (handoff
lines 65–150); and the book reviews in `docs/proposals/training-book-reviews.md`
(Juggernaut squat/bench/deadlift manuals, Meadows, Nippard's chest/back/
shoulder programmes, Helms's _Pyramid_). The first draft also took Ramos-Campo
2024, Robinson 2024 and Huiberts 2024 from the handoff (~line 611); the
2026-10-07 pass checked them with the rest of the source table. The owner's
coaching transcripts (Sebastian Oreb) are summarised separately in
`owner-lifting-sources.md`. This file reconciles them with the research in §4.6
and does not repeat them.

## 0. The findings that matter most for decisions

1. **Volume drives hypertrophy, with diminishing returns. Strength flattens
   on volume quickly and depends more on load, frequency and specificity.**
   STRONG for the shapes, MODERATE for the thresholds. Pelland 2025 [VF]: both
   outcomes rise with weekly sets (posterior probability 100%). Strength
   flattens far sooner: past roughly 3–4 fractional sets a week, extra sets
   add less than the smallest detectable effect (3.96%), though the fitted
   curve keeps rising slowly (about +1.1 percentage points from 8 to 20
   sets). Within one session, Remmert 2025 [VF] finds no detectable extra
   strength past about 2 direct sets, a detection point rather than a cap.
   Hypertrophy keeps rising slowly, with no detectable advantage past about 30
   fractional sets a week.
2. **ADR-0010's flip to 1:1 counting runs against the newest evidence.**
   MODERATE. Tropos has counted an indirect set as 1.0 since 2026-08-03
   (`SECONDARY_SET_WEIGHT = 1.0` in `volumeModel.ts`; ADR-0010's second
   addendum). Pelland 2025 [VF] tested three ways of counting a set against
   the outcomes. Its weekly fits favour fractional counting for both
   hypertrophy and strength. An exploratory fit put the best weight for an
   indirect set at about 0.38–0.39 of a direct set, and 0.5 fits well.
   Counting direct sets only fit weekly strength worst of the three. It is
   supported only per session, for strength (Remmert 2025 [VF]). So the
   evidence argues for returning to about 0.4–0.5. That is a question for
   ADR-0010 and the owner, not a code change. ADR-0010 rejected re-expressing
   its bands in 0.5 because that would be "arithmetic with no external
   check". Pelland's thresholds now provide that check.
3. **Twice a week per muscle is the right floor** (STRONG). At the same weekly
   volume, more sessions help strength more than size (MODERATE). In Pelland
   2025 [VF], strength gains relative to twice a week were 0.73 at once a
   week, 1.14 at three times and 1.22 at four, with nothing detectable past
   three. Three sessions against one added only +0.8% to muscle size, a
   small effect that may be negligible. Grgic 2018 [VF] found no frequency
   effect on strength once volume was equated, so the effect rests on
   Pelland alone. Pelland counted sessions per muscle, so applying it per
   _lift_ is an inference from specificity. Lift4 guarantees the
   twice-a-week floor. It does not guarantee frequent practice of each main
   lift.
4. **Effort: 1–3 reps in reserve on compounds, failure optional.**
   MODERATE–STRONG. Hypertrophy improves a little as sets end closer to
   failure. Strength does not (Robinson 2024 [VF]). Failure did not appear to
   harm strength either: where non-failure training gained more, its groups
   had done more sets, and the authors put the gap down to volume (Grgic
   2022 [VF]). Lift4's cue (2 reps to spare on compounds, the last set to the
   limit on isolations) matches the evidence.
5. **For a 1RM, heavy loads and practice of heavy low-rep sets matter**
   (STRONG for load, MODERATE for test practice). Lift4's Get stronger
   prescribes 5s at about 2 reps in reserve. That is about a 7RM effort: 83%
   of 1RM on the classic table, and about 85% on Nuzzo 2024's averages for
   the main lifts [VF], so it sits at the lighter end of the ≥80% consensus
   zone. It has no heavier exposure, no peak and no test, so it falls short
   for powerlifters.
6. **Periodization helps strength modestly (ES ~0.3–0.4) but not
   hypertrophy.** MODERATE. Lift4's heavier and lighter days for
   intermediates are the evidence-aligned kind of variation.
7. **Autoregulation by RPE or RIR does at least as well as fixed loading**
   (MODERATE). Lifters under-predict the reps they have left by about one
   (0.95 reps), and individuals spread around that with an SD of about 1.45
   reps (Halperin 2022 [VF]; MODERATE). Lift4's "the plan follows the weight
   lifted" and its 9.5 brake fit this.
8. **Deload evidence is thin** (WEAK). In practice athletes deload about every
   5–6 weeks by cutting sets and effort and keeping roughly the same
   exercises, and most also lighten multi-joint lifts (83.7%; Rogerson 2024
   [VF]). In the one trial, both groups gained strength, but a week fully off
   gave smaller lower-body strength gains than training straight through
   (Coleman 2024 [VF]: 3.6–4.5 kg less Smith-squat 1RM gain, depending on
   the model, with a 0.851 probability that training through did better).
   Lift4's lighter week (half the sets, same weights, every 4th trained week)
   is a defensible middle, though it keeps the weights that most athletes
   lighten.
9. **Expected progress.** Over 16 weeks, a novice gains about +20–30% on the
   main lifts, an intermediate +4–10% and an advanced lifter 0–5%. The
   intermediate and advanced figures are extrapolations with no direct
   anchor. Individual responses vary widely: the observed CV is about 50%,
   and true variation is somewhat less. A 100 kg bencher adding 10 kg in 16
   weeks is a stretch goal: roughly one lifter in five on a general
   programme, and one in three on a bench-specific block. It is not a typical
   outcome (WEAK, model-based).
10. **Running blunts lower-body strength, mainly in men and when running and
    lifting share a session, and blunts explosive strength. Upper-body
    strength and hypertrophy are barely touched.** MODERATE. Huiberts 2024
    [VF] found the lower-body cost in men (SMD −0.43), not in women, and no
    difference by strength-training status. Petré 2021 [VF] found it in
    trained people only when the two shared a session. One session a week at
    the same relative load maintains strength in younger adults (Spiering
    2021 [VA]). Lift4's race-build leg trim, taper weeks and spacing rules
    are well supported.

**The changes the evidence suggests most strongly** (detail in §6):

- (a) Put ADR-0010 back to the owner (the 1:1 flip landed 2026-08-03): the
  weekly evidence argues for returning to about 0.4–0.5 counting, for
  strength as well as hypertrophy judgements. It is a decision for the ADR,
  not a code change. Pelland's weekly fits do not support counting direct
  sets only for strength-lift tallies.
- (b) Give Get stronger a strength track:
  - a frequency floor for each main lift;
  - heavier exposure (an optional top set at RPE 7–9);
  - a dated test or meet using the race-week machinery.
- (c) Let the simulation compare intermediate strength progression rules: the
  current session-to-session step, a weekly step, and a step gated on logged
  effort.
- (d) Scale the two-miss drop by level, for example 5% for advanced lifters.
- (e) Default to small plates for light lifts (overhead press, many women's
  presses).
- (f) Offer priority muscles above the volume ceiling to time-rich
  hypertrophy lifters.
- (g) Keep an e1RM trend in the back end, for stall detection and expectation
  copy, not as a %1RM prescription.

## 1. Books and coaching systems: what each contributes, and its limit

All of this is coaching convention unless a trial is cited. The useful
question for each source is which of its ideas has research behind it.

**Sheiko (_Powerlifting: Foundations and Methods_, 2018) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ frequent practice of the competition lifts at submaximal
  loads: mostly ~70–85%, few reps per set, many sets, rarely near failure,
  in monthly loading waves, with bench often trained three or more times a
  week [U]. Volume is counted as the number of lifts at 50% of 1RM or more,
  with their average intensity, which typically sits at about 70% (±2%).
- _Research fit:_ consistent with frequency mattering for strength (Pelland
  2025 [VF], MODERATE) and with failure being unnecessary for strength
  (Robinson 2024 [VF]).
- _Limit:_ built for competitive lifters in a sport-school system. The
  templates are heavy on volume and lightly individualised.
- _Tropos:_ supports a per-lift frequency floor on the strength goal, and a
  count of lifts at ≥70% as a possible strength-volume metric. The ≥70%
  threshold is a Tropos variant: Sheiko counts lifts from 50%, and 70% is
  his typical average intensity.

**Tuchscherer, Reactive Training Systems (2008) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ the RPE scale anchored to reps in reserve, which became the
  research RIR-RPE scale (Zourdos 2016 [VF]; Helms 2016 [VF]). In the 2008
  manual an 8 still meant 2–4 reps left, so the one-to-one anchoring came
  later. A top set at a target RPE sets the day's load. Back-off volume
  follows a load drop [U] or a "fatigue percentage": keep doing sets until
  performance falls by a set margin (300×3 @9 then 285×3 @9 is 5% fatigue).
  The manual's chart of RPE and reps against %1RM is only a rough guide to
  how heavy you have been training, and it says not to use it to derive a
  max. Tracking e1RM from rated sets is later RTS practice.
- _Limit:_ everything depends on rating accuracy. Lifters under-predict the
  reps they have left by about one (0.95 reps), individuals spread around
  that with an SD of about 1.45 reps, and accuracy is worse past 12 reps
  (Halperin 2022 [VF]). Halperin found no effect of training status. The
  claim that novices rate worse rests on one study of 1RM ratings on one
  lift in 29 people (Zourdos 2016 [VF]). The stopping rules have not been
  validated.
- _Tropos:_ the blueprint for an advanced, opt-in strength mode. Later RTS
  practice, not the 2008 manual, is the precedent for an e1RM signal from
  rated sets.

**Juggernaut Method 2.0 (C.W. Smith) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ 16 weeks of rep waves (10s, 8s, 5s, 3s). Each 4-week wave
  runs accumulation, intensification, realisation (an AMRAP) and a deload.
  The AMRAP result resets the training max, which is a simple performance
  feedback loop.
- _Limit:_ the all-out AMRAP comes once a wave, in the realisation week. The
  accumulation and intensification weeks end on "+" sets with reps in
  reserve, and the deload has none. So the fatigue and form risk for novices
  is one all-out set per lift every fourth week, and the rules are
  arbitrary.
- _Tropos:_ an AMRAP-driven max update is a known pattern. Tropos's
  non-adoption of AMRAP by default stands. The repo's book review already
  covers the Juggernaut manuals.

**Wendler, _5/3/1 Forever_ (2017) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ train from a "training max" below the true max, which is
  conservative by construction (like working at 2+ reps in reserve). Raise it
  in small fixed steps each cycle. Run three-week cycles grouped into
  "leader" (building) and "anchor" (realising) blocks, with supplemental
  volume templates. A deload or training-max test week (the "7th Week
  Protocol") ends a block, typically after two leader cycles and again after
  the anchor, not after every cycle. Secondary accounts describe two resets
  in Forever: in the test, fewer than 3 reps at the training max means the
  training max comes down, re-estimated from that set; and programmed
  "5 forward, 3 back" resets.
- _Limit:_ the progression rate is the same whatever the response, and the
  AMRAP "plus" sets invite grinding.
- _Tropos:_ the strongest coaching precedent for slow, sustainable
  intermediate progression, and for resets. No fixed reset of about 10% was
  found in Forever [U]: the 10% reset (start again from 90% of the training
  max) is attributed to original 5/3/1. Either way it is CONVENTION.

**Rippetoe & Baker, _Practical Programming for Strength Training_ (3rd ed., 2014) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ defines training age by how quickly a lifter recovers from
  and adapts to a training stress. A novice progresses session to session, an
  intermediate week to week, an advanced lifter month to month or slower.
  Programme complexity should follow that.
- _Limit:_ anecdotal, barbell-centric, strength-only, and indifferent to
  hypertrophy evidence.
- _Tropos:_ the most useful operational definition of level for an app,
  because it can be detected from data as the slope of the e1RM trend against
  noise. It also exposes a Lift4 tension: intermediate strength main lifts
  still step session to session (§6, L15).

**Texas Method and Madcow 5×5 [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ intermediate weekly progression. The Texas Method uses a
  volume day, a light day and an intensity day that sets a new rep record.
  Madcow ramps to a top set of five and adds a heavier triple later in the
  week, progressing about weekly. Madcow repeats a missed weight the next
  week, and goes back a few weeks only after several weeks without
  progress. How the Texas Method handles a stall could not be confirmed [U].
- _Limit:_ the weekly intensity day grinds, and neither has been trialled.
- _Tropos:_ the precedent for a "weekly step" rule for intermediates.

**GZCL (Cody Lefever) [VF].**

- _Contribution:_ three tiers. Tier 1 is heavy low-rep main lifts, tier 2
  moderate-load secondary lifts, tier 3 higher-rep accessories, with the rep
  share growing down the tiers.
- _Limit:_ published as a blog, with no trials.
- _Tropos:_ Lift4's role table (main lifts, other compounds, isolations) is
  effectively a tier system. Lift4 already matches it.

**Candito 6-Week Strength Program [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ a short block sequence from conditioning and hypertrophy
  through heavy acclimation to a test week. It is an accessible taste of
  block periodization.
- _Limit:_ generic percentages and a test every six weeks.
- _Tropos:_ a model for an optional six-week strength block that ends in a
  test.

**Calgary Barbell (Bryce Krawczyk) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ modern powerlifting templates that combine %1RM and RPE.
  Most loads are a percentage of 1RM: weeks 1–11 of the 16-week programme
  are %1RM throughout. RPE top sets appear only in its last phase (weeks
  12–15: 1×3, then 1×2, then 1×1 at RPE 8), and even there the back-offs are
  a fixed %1RM (week 12 squat: 1×3 @8, then 6×5 at 62%), never a percentage
  of the top set. It also uses competition-specific variations (pauses,
  pins), and its 8- and 16-week blocks end in a peak.
- _Limit:_ meet-focused, and assumes the lifter can rate RPE.
- _Tropos:_ the blueprint for a "meet mode".

**Stronger By Science (Nuckols and colleagues) [VF].**

- _Contribution:_ plain-language syntheses of the volume, frequency and
  failure research. Programmes adjust the training max automatically from the
  last set's reps (AMRAP) or from reps in reserve.
- _Limit:_ the programmes themselves have not been trialled.
- _Tropos:_ the precedent for automatic max updates from logged sets.

**Westside conjugate (Simmons, 2007) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ rotate the max-effort exercise variation weekly to avoid
  staleness, add dynamic-effort speed days with bands and chains, and pick
  accessories for weak points.
- _Limit:_ developed with equipped and often enhanced lifters. Weekly maxes
  are too demanding for consumers, and there is little evidence for raw
  lifters or novices.
- _Tropos:_ variation for a stall and a weak-point emphasis belong as user
  choices, not defaults.

**Prilepin's chart (1975, via Laputin & Oleshko) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ by intensity zone, a range of reps per set and of total reps
  per session, drawn from Soviet weightlifters.
- _Limit:_ the Olympic lifts are explosive and technical, unlike
  powerlifting or hypertrophy work. The ranges were never validated, and
  presenting them as "optimal" totals overstates them.
- _Tropos:_ a rough sanity check on heavy-single volume at most. Non-adopted
  as a rule (§7).

**Bompa & Buzzichelli, _Periodization_ (6th ed., 2019) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ the vocabulary of macro-, meso- and microcycles, and phase
  sequences (anatomical adaptation, hypertrophy, maximal strength,
  conversion, maintenance, transition), plus taper principles.
- _Limit:_ theory-heavy and rooted in classical periodization. Specific phase
  structures and lengths have little trial support. ACSM 2026 [VF] found that
  periodization did not consistently change outcomes in healthy adults, and
  could not determine its effect on strength.
- _Tropos:_ phase names for a race or meet calendar, without prescriptive
  phase lengths.

**Stone, Stone & Sands, _Principles and Practice of Resistance Training_
(2007) [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ block periodization (concentrated loading then
  realisation), fitness–fatigue reasoning and monitoring. The ETSU group's
  taper work (Travis 2020 [VF]) comes from this lineage.
- _Limit:_ written for athletes.
- _Tropos:_ the theoretical basis for the strength track's blocks and tapers.

**PHUL, PHAT and Nippard's powerbuilding programmes [VA].**

- _Contribution:_ powerbuilding combines heavy low-rep compound work ("power"
  days or top sets) with hypertrophy volume ("pump" days or accessories).
  PHUL uses four upper/lower days. PHAT uses five days, two power and three
  hypertrophy. Nippard's versions add RPE targets and top sets, with lighter
  back-off sets after them. The product pages say "high effort top sets";
  user transcriptions of the programme show the back-offs.
- _Limit:_ commercial and never trialled. PHAT's volume is high for
  consumers.
- _Tropos:_ the shape of a "powerbuilding" dial: strength-style main lifts
  with hypertrophy-style accessories and volume.

**Helms et al., natural bodybuilding recommendations (training 2015 [VA];
nutrition 2014 [VF]).**

- _Contribution:_ train each muscle group at least twice a week, and more
  often when volume is high so that no one session runs too long. Do roughly
  40–70 reps per muscle group per session, possibly more for advanced
  bodybuilders. Most reps fall in the 6–12 range at 70–80% of 1RM, with some
  heavier work. Rest long enough to keep performance up, and keep failure
  mainly to single-joint, higher-rep work. Whether to keep training loads
  and effort through a cut is not stated in the 2015 abstract [U]. The 2014
  paper gives no training advice. For nutrition it advises losing slowly and
  eating high protein, and, if training performance drops, taking fewer
  calories from fat and more from carbohydrate.
- _Limit:_ written for contest preparation, before the newer volume
  meta-regressions.
- _Tropos:_ Helms 2015 prescribes a normal hypertrophy dose during contest
  prep, which fits Lift4's "Lose fat = Build muscle + a nutrition cut".
  Whether it supports retiring the set cut that used to come with a diet
  (§6, L30) cannot be confirmed without the full text [U].

## 2. Research by topic

### 2.1 Weekly volume

**Hypertrophy.** Growth rises with weekly sets, with diminishing returns
(STRONG).

- Schoenfeld 2017 [VA]: each extra weekly set added about 0.37 percentage
  points of growth (ES ~0.023 per set) over the range studied. It counts
  every set as 1, so do not mix it with Pelland's fractional curve.
- Pelland 2025 [VF]: the slope of volume on hypertrophy was positive with
  posterior probability 100%, and returns diminished. Counted in fractional
  sets, the slope is 0.24% per set (95% CrI 0.15–0.33) at the mean of 12.25
  sets a week.
- Pelland 2025 [VF]: the smallest detectable dose is about 4 fractional sets a
  week. Each further detectable gain needs about 6 more sets in the 5–10
  range, rising to about 12.5 more at 30–42. No higher volume is more than 50%
  likely to beat 31 fractional sets by more than the smallest detectable
  effect (2.05%). That figure comes from the authors' OSF table and from
  Remmert 2025, not from the abstract. It is a detection point, not a
  plateau: the fitted curve keeps rising.
- Baz-Valle 2022 [VF]: 12–20 weekly sets did as well as more than 20 for the
  quadriceps and biceps. More than 20 helped only the triceps.
- Schoenfeld 2019 [VF]: 34 trained men did 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise: 6,
  18 or 30 weekly sets per muscle for the arms, and 9, 27 or 45 for the legs.
  Growth was graded, but the only significant contrasts were the 5-set group
  against the 1-set group, at three of four sites. Evidence for the steps in
  between was weak or absent, and the elbow extensors did not differ. 1RM
  did not differ between groups.

**Strength.** Strength rises with volume, but flattens much sooner (MODERATE).

- Pelland 2025 [VF]: diminishing returns are "considerably more pronounced"
  for strength. Counted in fractional sets, not direct sets, the last
  detectable step comes at 3–4 sets a week. From 5 sets up, further sets do
  not consistently add a gain above the smallest detectable effect (3.96%).
  No higher volume is more than 50% likely to beat 3 fractional sets by that
  margin (computed from the authors' OSF table; Remmert 2025 quotes ~3).
- Nuckols 2025 (Stronger By Science) [VF] reports the same plateau, at about
  4–5 weekly fractional sets. It then argues the plateau should not be read as
  strength saturating. In trained lifters with paired strength and size
  measures, the highest-volume groups (27.3 sets a week on average) gained
  more strength than groups at about 19 sets in 7 of 9 comparisons. The
  article reports that two of Pelland's co-authors put the plateau down to
  lower specificity in the high-volume studies.
- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 [VA]: even one set per exercise, taken to
  failure one to three times a week, raised squat and bench 1RM in trained
  men over 8–12 weeks (by 12.1 kg, 8.2–16.0), though sub-optimally.

**Product implications.**

- Hypertrophy: the first ~10 fractional sets per muscle per week buy most of
  what 20 sets buy (about 4.2% against 6.5% above control in Pelland's fit).
  Going from 10 to 20 buys a smaller, real increment. Past about 30, further
  gains are hard to detect and the time cost is high, though the fitted curve
  keeps rising.
- Strength: 3–4 fractional sets per lift per week capture most of the
  detectable volume effect. Additional sets serve hypertrophy, technique and
  skill, and hypertrophy raises the long-term ceiling. In trained lifters,
  higher volumes still gained more strength in most comparisons, so the
  plateau is not proof of saturation.

### 2.2 How to count a set (the ADR-0010 question)

Pelland 2025 [VF] quantified volume three ways:

- **total**: an indirect set counts 1;
- **fractional**: an indirect set counts 0.5;
- **direct**: an indirect set counts 0.

It then fitted each against the outcomes. Fractional counting fitted best for
both hypertrophy and strength, for weekly volume and for frequency. For weekly
strength, direct-only counting fitted worst of the three. Swept from 0 to
100%, the best indirect weight was about 38% of a direct set for hypertrophy
and 39% for strength. Direct-only counting fitted strength best only per
session, in Remmert 2025 [VF]. MODERATE: one well-built model comparison.

ADR-0010 counted an indirect set at 0.5 when it was written. It records "1:1
is correct" as settled, because the bands it uses came from 1:1
meta-analyses. It rejected option 2 (keep 0.5 and re-derive the bands)
because the re-derivation would be "arithmetic with no external check". Its
first 2026-08-03 addendum held the flip back until a per-head taxonomy split,
because at 1:1 the shoulder and core buckets absorbed every press and
compound as full sets. Its second addendum records that the split landed,
and the flip with it. Tropos has counted an indirect set as 1.0 since
2026-08-03 (`SECONDARY_SET_WEIGHT = 1.0` in `volumeModel.ts`).

Pelland's weekly fits are the external check the ADR lacked. They favour
fractional counting over 1.0 for both outcomes, including strength: the best
weight was about 0.38–0.39, and 0.5 fits well. Direct-only counting fit
weekly strength worst; it is supported only per session (Remmert). So the
evidence argues for returning to about 0.4–0.5. **Recommendation:** put that
question to the owner through ADR-0010. It is not a code change. If the ADR
is re-opened:

- Re-derive the bands in fractional currency from Pelland's curves.
- Count direct sets only for a per-session check on a strength goal's main
  lift (Remmert). For weekly strength volume, the evidence favours fractional
  counting.

### 2.3 Per-session volume

Remmert et al. 2025 (preprint) [VF]: within one session, gains stop being
detectable past about **11 fractional sets per muscle** for hypertrophy, or
about **2 direct sets** for strength. These are detection points, not caps:
gains continue past them, more slowly. WEAK–MODERATE (preprint). This suggests
that frequency starts to matter once weekly volume is high: a muscle needing
20 sets may be better served by two or three sessions than by one. But a
re-analysis of Pelland's data (Boutagy & McKean 2026, preprint) [VA] found
that splitting a weekly volume into more sessions made no detectable
difference to hypertrophy, though it helped strength. So the case for
splitting is weak for size. Lift4's twice a week with three sets per exercise
stays far below these points.

### 2.4 Frequency

- **Per muscle, for hypertrophy.** In Schoenfeld 2016 [VA], higher per-muscle
  frequency beat lower (ES 0.49 vs 0.30). That is a binary higher-vs-lower
  contrast, not once against twice; "twice a week beats once" is the authors'
  conclusion from it. At least 7 of its 10 trials equated weekly volume.
  Later analyses found that with volume equated, frequency makes no
  difference (Schoenfeld 2019 [VA]; Ramos-Campo 2024 [VA]). Pelland 2025 [VF]
  put the slope at 0.32% per session (95% CrI −0.14 to 0.82), which is
  compatible with no effect. STRONG.
- **For strength.** Higher frequency raised 1RM, largely through added volume
  (Grgic 2018 [VF]). Pelland 2025 [VF] found a frequency effect on strength
  even after adjusting for volume: 3.27% per session. Relative to twice a
  week, once gave 0.73, three times 1.14 and four times 1.22, with nothing
  more detectable past three. Part of this is likely practice of the tested
  lift. MODERATE.
- **Consensus.** ACSM 2026 [VF]: resistance training at least twice a week,
  with all major muscle groups engaged. It does not say each muscle twice a
  week.
- **Product implications.** "Every muscle twice a week" is a sensible default,
  not a proven floor: at equal volume, frequency made no detectable difference
  to size. For strength users, the frequency that matters is per _lift_:
  practice of the specific movement.

#### Verified parameters (2026-10-07)

Checked against primary sources on 7 October 2026. [VF] here means read in the preprint full text or the authors' OSF files. [VA] means abstract only. [C] means computed in this check from the authors' published tables.

**Sources and status**

- Pelland JC et al. _Sports Med_ 2026;56(2):481–505 (online 4 Dec 2025). PMID 41343037. doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. The published full text is closed and was not read. The numbers below come from the preprint (SportRxiv 460 v2, doi:10.51224/srxiv.460) and its OSF files (osf.io/6z3xu). The published abstract matches the preprint's.
- Remmert JF et al. "Is there too much of a good thing?" SportRxiv 537 v1, doi:10.51224/srxiv.537 (March 2025). It is still a preprint, with no journal version in PubMed, Europe PMC or Crossref. Two ACSM 2026 conference abstracts exist (_MSSE_ 58(10S)), and their text was not read.
- Data: 67 studies and 2,058 people, 79% men, mean age 25, mean 10.4 weeks. The hypertrophy models used 35 studies (220 effects), and the strength models 66 studies (490 effects). Median weekly fractional sets: 10.5 for hypertrophy, 6 for strength. Few studies ran past ~25 fractional sets a week.
- Smallest detectable effect (SDES): 2.05% for hypertrophy, 3.96% for strength. Every threshold below is measured against it. [VF]

**How to count a set** [VF]

- Pelland, weekly volume: fractional counting (indirect set = 0.5) fitted best for both outcomes. In 2×ln(BF), fractional beat total by 9.48 (hypertrophy) and 18.21 (strength). It beat direct by 10.29 and 45.96. Direct-only was the worst fit for strength.
- Pelland, frequency: fractional won again (9.96 and 10.82 for hypertrophy; 31.27 and 54.84 for strength).
- The authors also tried every indirect weight from 0 to 100%. For weekly volume, fit peaked at about 38% (hypertrophy) and 39% (strength) (OSF rm4xy). 0.5 sits near the peak, and 1.0 fits hypertrophy worse.
- Remmert, per session: fractional was best for hypertrophy, though only weakly over direct (0.96). Direct was best for strength (9.87 over fractional). The best per-session indirect weight for strength was about 16%.
- So "direct counting fits strength best" holds per session (Remmert), not per week (Pelland).
- For ADR-0010: the weekly evidence favours about 0.4–0.5 over 1.0 for both outcomes. Today the code counts 1.0 (`SECONDARY_SET_WEIGHT`, `volumeModel.ts`). Pelland's "indirect" means a synergist for hypertrophy (triceps in pressing, biceps in rows). For strength it means any exercise other than the tested lift. Check that Tropos's secondary map follows the same rule before using the weight.

**Weekly volume and hypertrophy (H)** [VF][C]

- Best fit: square root, not log. ln(response ratio) ~ √(V+1), plus frequency, weeks and training status.
- Gain above control at the dataset's mean duration ≈ 100·(e^(0.0177·(√(V+1) − 1)) − 1)%. This is reconstructed from the OSF table and matches every row.
- Points: 4 sets 2.2%, 10 sets 4.2%, 20 sets 6.5%, 30 sets 8.4%, 40 sets 10.0%.
- The slope is 0.24% per set (95% CrI 0.15–0.33) at the mean of 12.25 sets. The average slope falls from 0.33% per set (4–10 sets) to 0.24% (10–20), 0.19% (20–30) and 0.16% (30–40).
- The smallest detectable dose is 4 fractional sets a week. Each further detectable step needs about 6 more sets (5–10), 8.5 (11–18), 10.75 (19–29) and 12.5 (30–42). There is too little data past 43 sets.
- No higher volume is more than 50% likely to beat 31 sets by more than the SDES [C, OSF 2c3bt; Remmert quotes ~31]. That is a detection point, not a plateau: the fitted curve keeps rising.
- Schoenfeld 2017's 0.37% per set (ES 0.023; PMID 27433992) counts every set as 1. Do not mix it with this curve.

**Weekly volume and strength (D)** [VF][C]

- Best fit: reciprocal. Gain above control ≈ 100·(e^(0.1464·(1 − 1/(V+1))) − 1)%, with a ceiling of about 15.8%.
- Points: 1 set 7.6%, 2 sets 10.3%, 3 sets 11.6%, 5 sets 13.0%, 8 sets 13.9%, 20 sets 15.0%.
- Relative to 8 sets: 1 set 0.55, 3 sets 0.84, 5 sets 0.93, 6 sets 0.96, 10 sets 1.02, 20 sets 1.08.
- These are fractional sets, not direct sets. The smallest detectable dose is 1 set. Detectable steps come at 2 sets and at 3–4 sets, and none from 5 sets up. No higher volume is more than 50% likely to beat 3 sets by more than the SDES [C; Remmert quotes ~3].
- The authors expect the 1-set effect is inflated by learning an unfamiliar test.

**Frequency at equal volume (Fq)** [VF][C]

- Hypertrophy: 0.32% per session (95% CrI −0.14 to 0.82), with a 91.3% chance the slope is above zero. That is compatible with no effect. Three sessions against one: +0.8% (−0.3 to 2.1). Use Fq = 1.0.
- Strength: 3.27% per session (2.74–3.84), reciprocal. Gain above control at mean volume: 12.7% at 1×, 17.3% at 2×, 19.7% at 3×, 21.1% at 4×. Relative to 2×, that is 0.73, 1.0, 1.14 and 1.22. Nothing more is detectable past 3 sessions.
- Frequency here counts an indirect session as 0.5. Part of the strength effect is likely practice of the tested lift.

**Per-session volume** [VF]

- Hypertrophy: best fit linear-log, 0.39% per set (0.20–0.58). The detection point is ~11 fractional sets per session (≈ 8 direct, ≈ 14 total). The data averaged 6 fractional sets per session.
- Strength: best fit reciprocal on direct sets, 1.23% per set (0.94–1.52). The detection point is ~2 direct sets per session (≈ 3.5 fractional, ≈ 5 total).
- These are detection points, not limits. Gains continue past them, more slowly.
- A re-analysis of Pelland's own data (Boutagy & McKean, SportRxiv 1100, 6 October 2026) [VA] found that splitting one weekly volume into more sessions made no detectable difference to hypertrophy, but helped strength. Treat the 11-set figure as weak evidence for splitting volume for size.

**Other §2.1–2.4 sources** [VA unless marked]

- Baz-Valle 2022 (PMID 35291645): 6 studies meta-analysed. Moving from 12–20 to more than 20 sets helped the triceps (p = 0.01), but not the quadriceps (p = 0.19) or biceps (p = 0.59). The DOI is confirmed.
- Schoenfeld 2019, _MSSE_ (PMID 30153194, full text) [VF]: 6, 18 and 30 weekly sets for the arms; 9, 27 and 45 for the legs. Growth was graded, and 1RM did not differ.
- Schoenfeld 2016 (PMID 27102172): ES 0.49 against 0.30. Schoenfeld 2019, _J Sports Sci_ (PMID 30558493): 25 studies, no effect when volume is equated.
- Grgic 2018 (PMID 29470825): ES 0.74, 0.82, 0.93 and 1.08 for 1, 2, 3 and 4+ sessions a week; p = 0.421 when volume is equated.
- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 (PMID 31797219): one set 1–3 times a week raised 1RM in trained men, by 12.1 kg (8.2–16.0).

**Still unverified**

- Remmert's two 2026 conference abstracts.
- Schoenfeld 2016's 6.8% against 3.7%, which is not in the abstract.
- The E, N, A_h, C_h, I, γ and B\* terms in §4.4 were outside this check. Each carries its own source and grade in §4.4, and E is checked in §2.13's block.

### 2.5 Load and the repetition continuum

STRONG.

- 1RM gains favour heavy loads (Schoenfeld 2017 [VA]).
- Lopez 2021 [VF]: high (≤8RM) and moderate (9–15RM) loads beat low loads
  (>15RM) for strength (SMD 0.51 and 0.34, as amended by the 2022
  corrigendum). High beat moderate by a further 0.16 (95% CI −0.05 to 0.38),
  which was not significant.
- Hypertrophy is similar across a wide range of loads, from about 30% of 1RM
  upward, when sets are taken to failure (Schoenfeld 2017 [VA]; Lopez 2021
  [VF]; Schoenfeld 2021 [VF]). Every included study took sets to failure. At
  light loads, stopping short costs growth: in Lasevicius 2019, cited by
  Schoenfeld 2021, 30% of 1RM not taken to failure fell behind 30% to failure.
  No source sets an upper bound, though heavy loads need more sets for the
  same growth (Schoenfeld 2021).
- Currier 2023 [VF]: the top-ranked strength prescription was heavy (>80% of
  1RM), multiple sets, three times a week. ACSM 2026 [VF]: ≥80% of 1RM, 2–3
  sets, early in the session, at least twice a week.
- **Product implications.** Rep ranges are a tool for time, comfort and
  joint load, not a switch between "hypertrophy" and "endurance". Light-load
  sets need a high effort to match heavier ones; the trials took them to
  failure. Strength goals need most main-lift work at ≥75–80% of 1RM.

### 2.6 Proximity to failure

- Failure is not needed for hypertrophy, and the relationship is probably
  non-linear (Refalo 2023 [VF]).
- Robinson 2024 [VF]: estimated reps in reserve moderated hypertrophy
  (closer to failure, more growth) but not strength.
- Grgic 2022 [VF]:
  - no overall difference between failure and non-failure;
  - in studies that did not equate volume, non-failure groups gained more
    strength (ES −0.32), but they mostly did more sets. The authors put the
    gap down to volume, not failure. With volume equated, the ES was 0.01;
  - in trained lifters, failure was slightly better for growth (ES 0.15).
- Refalo 2024 [VA]: a within-participant trial in trained lifters found
  similar growth at 0 and 1–2 reps in reserve.
- Hickmott 2022 [VF]: more velocity loss within a set (sets taken closer to
  failure) gave more CSA. Less velocity loss gave more 1RM.
- ACSM 2026 [VF]: training to failure did not consistently affect outcomes.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE–STRONG. Compounds at 1–3 reps in
  reserve. Isolations can go to failure on the last set. Strength work stays
  1–3 reps from failure.

### 2.7 Rest between sets

- Schoenfeld 2016 [VA]: 3 minutes beat 1 minute for squat and bench 1RM in
  trained men. Thickness was greater only at the anterior quadriceps, with a
  trend at the triceps and no difference at the elbow flexors or the lateral
  thigh. Volume load was not equated.
- Singer 2024 [VF]: a small growth benefit from resting more than ~60 s. In
  four categories, the central estimates were 0.47 (short), 0.65
  (intermediate), 0.55 (long) and 0.50 (very long), so the benefit does not
  keep climbing.
- Grgic 2018 [VA]: trained lifters do better on strength with rests over two
  minutes.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. Rest about 2–3 minutes on heavy
  compounds and at least 60–90 seconds on isolations. Shorter rests cost a
  little hypertrophy and a fair amount of load.

### 2.8 Periodization

- Williams 2017 [VF]: periodized beat non-periodized for 1RM (ES 0.43), with
  undulating programmes favoured and larger effects in untrained people.
- Moesgaard 2022 [VA]: with volume equated, periodization helped 1RM
  (ES 0.31) but not hypertrophy. Undulating beat linear for 1RM (ES 0.31,
  95% CI 0.02 to 0.61), an edge seen in trained participants (ES 0.61) but
  not untrained ones (ES 0.06).
- Grgic 2017 [VF]: linear and daily undulating gave the same hypertrophy.
- ACSM 2026 [VF]: periodization did not consistently affect outcomes for the
  average adult, and its effect on strength "could not be determined".
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE for strength, a consistent null for
  hypertrophy. Planned variation in load and reps (heavier and lighter days,
  or blocks) is worth having on strength goals. Hypertrophy needs no periodized
  structure beyond managing fatigue.

### 2.9 Autoregulation, RPE and RIR

- Helms 2018 [VF]: in trained men over 8 weeks, RPE-chosen loads matched or
  slightly beat fixed percentages. Bench gained 10.7 vs 9.6 kg and squat 17.1
  vs 13.9 kg.
- Graham & Cleather 2019 [VF]: RIR-chosen squat loads beat fixed percentages
  over 12 weeks.
- Hickmott 2022 [VF]: autoregulated load prescription was not significantly
  different from percentage-based (+2.1 kg, 95% CI −0.3 to 4.5).
- Larsen 2021 [VF] agrees.
- Zourdos 2016 [VF]: RPE tracks bar velocity. Experienced squatters rated a
  true 1RM closer to 10 than novices did (9.80 vs 8.96). Accuracy below 1RM
  was not tested.
- Halperin 2022 [VF]: people under-predict the reps they have left by about
  0.95 (95% CI 0.17–1.73), and differ from each other by an SD of 1.45 reps.
  Accuracy is better close to failure and below 12 reps. Training status made
  no difference.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. Letting performance set the next load
  is evidence-aligned. Effort ratings help, but they are estimates. People
  under-call by about 1 rep on average, individuals differ by about 1.5 reps,
  and accuracy is worse in long sets. Halperin found no effect of training
  status; the concern about novices rests on one squat study (Zourdos 2016).
  Use ratings as brakes and for advanced users, not as a mandatory input.

### 2.10 Specificity of the 1RM

- Mattocks 2017 [VA]: in untrained people, practising the 1RM alone produced
  1RM gains equal to four sets of 8–12RM to failure, though less growth.
- Wolf 2023 [VA]: strength is range-of-motion specific.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. A meaningful part of a 1RM gain is
  skill at heavy loads. Two consequences:
  - A lifter who trains only 8–12 reps will under-express their strength in a
    1RM, and gains in RCTs that include repeated testing partly reflect test
    practice.
  - A strength user who wants a better 1RM needs some practice at heavy,
    low-rep loads. Mattocks supports only that direction: in untrained
    adults, single maximal attempts alone raised the 1RM. The dose (singles,
    doubles and triples at about RPE 7–9, in the last 4–6 weeks before a
    test) is CONVENTION; the trial did not test it.

### 2.11 Minimal dose and maintenance

- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 [VA]: in trained men, one set per exercise taken
  to failure raised squat and bench 1RM over 8–12 weeks, though
  sub-optimally. The effect on the deadlift is unclear. The authors'
  suggested minimum is one set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% of 1RM, two or three
  times a week.
- Spiering 2021 [VA]: in younger adults, strength and size are kept for up to
  32 weeks on one session a week with one set per exercise, as long as
  intensity is kept. Older adults may need up to two sessions a week and two
  or three sets per exercise to keep muscle size.
- Bickel 2011 [VF]: after 16 weeks of training, 32 weeks at one third of the
  dose (1 day × 3 sets) or one ninth (1 day × 1 set) kept 1RM and added
  7–8%. Stopping lost 7% after 32 weeks but stayed 23% above baseline. Fibre
  size held in young adults at both doses (more growth at one third), but at
  neither dose in 60–75-year-olds.
- Rønnestad 2010 [VA]: one heavy session a week kept strength gains in
  cyclists in season.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. Intensity is what keeps strength.
  Volume can fall by two thirds or more. Older adults need more to keep
  muscle size than to keep strength. This is the evidence base for:
  - Lift4's race-build leg trim;
  - "Short on time?" sessions;
  - maintenance volume for muscles that are not a priority.

### 2.12 Deloads

- Bell 2023 Delphi [VF]: defines a deload as "a period of reduced training
  stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue,
  promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training". The
  panel agreed separately that a deload can be pre-planned or autoregulated.
  It agreed universally that volume should come down. Intensity may be kept
  (only if volume drops), lowered or even raised. The panel set no order and
  no size.
- Rogerson 2024 [VF], a survey of 246 strength and physique athletes:
  - deloads last about 6.4 ± 1.7 days, every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks;
  - fewer weekly sets (78.9%), fewer reps per set (52.8%), less effort
    (84.9%) and lighter loads on multi-joint lifts (83.7%; how much lighter
    was not asked), with the same session count (63.0%) and the same
    multi-joint exercises (70.3%);
  - taken pre-planned (47.2%), autoregulated when performance stalls or
    joints ache (13.4%), or both (39.4%).
- Coleman 2024 [VF], the only RCT (39 of 50 finished): a week of complete rest
  in the middle of nine weeks left lower-body strength gains slightly smaller
  than training straight through, and did not change hypertrophy. Smith-squat
  1RM ended 3.6–4.5 kg below continuous training, depending on the model, with
  credible intervals that include zero. Both groups still gained strength.
- **Grade and implications.** WEAK: one RCT, one Delphi, one survey. The
  Delphi and the survey agree on fewer sets and less effort. Load may be
  kept or lowered, and most athletes lower it on multi-joint lifts. A week
  fully off is not a "deload". No source supports a particular cadence as physiology.

### 2.13 Tapering and peaking

- Pritchard 2016 [VF], interviews with 11 elite New Zealand powerlifters:
  - volume peaked about 5 weeks out (5.2 ± 1.7) and average intensity about
    2 weeks out (1.9 ± 0.8);
  - the taper lasted 2.4 ± 0.9 weeks and cut volume by about 59%;
  - the last training session of any kind came 3.7 ± 1.6 days before the
    meet (8 of 11 lifters at 3 days), in a final week with both volume and
    intensity cut;
  - the last heavy sessions came earlier. The author's thesis copy of the
    manuscript (Table 4.3) gives 8.0 ± 2.9 days out for the squat, 7.3 ± 2.7
    for the bench and 10.9 ± 4.0 for the deadlift;
  - accessories were dropped about 2 weeks out;
  - deadlifts were said to need longer to recover from;
  - a week or more fully off was felt to go badly.
- Travis 2020 [VF]: cut volume by 30–70%, keep intensity at or above 85% of
  1RM or lower it, use a step or exponential taper over 1–2 weeks, then take
  2–7 days fully off. Cuts of 30–50% looked better than larger ones for the
  squat and bench. Powerlifting-specific trials are scarce.
- Pritchard 2015 [VF], a review, read in the author's thesis copy: cut volume
  by 30–70% over 1–4 weeks, keep or slightly raise intensity, and stop
  training 2–7 days out.
- Bosquet 2007 [VA]: endurance tapers of about two weeks, cutting volume by
  41–60% with intensity and frequency kept.
- **Grade and implications.** WEAK–MODERATE. For a strength test or meet, cut
  volume by about 30–50% (up to 70%) over 1–2 weeks with intensity kept, then
  take 2–7 days off. Elite lifters' last session of any kind came about 3–4
  days out, and their last heavy sets about 1–1.5 weeks out, earlier for the
  deadlift. Travis's guideline allows heavy work up to the end of the taper.
  For a race, Lift4's two lighter weeks and rest days fit the same logic.

### 2.14 Training at long muscle lengths

- Maeo 2021 [VF]: the seated leg curl, which trains the hamstrings at long
  length, grew them more than the prone curl.
- Maeo 2023 [VA]: the overhead triceps extension produced about 1.4× the
  triceps growth of the pushdown (19.9% vs 13.9%; long head 28.5% vs 19.6%).
- Kassiano 2023 [VA]: calf partials at long lengths beat full ROM for the
  medial gastrocnemius (+15.2% vs +6.7%). The full-range group reached the
  same stretched position but grew less.
- Pedrosa 2022 [VA]: long-length partials at least matched full ROM for knee
  extension.
- Wolf 2023 [VA]: full or long ROM is slightly better for most outcomes.
- Strey 2026 [VA], 8 studies: long-length partials beat short-length partials
  for hypertrophy (ES 0.28).
- Wolf et al.'s 2025 systematic review [VF]: longer-length training
  consistently grows more.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE for _choosing_ exercises that load
  the muscle at long length: the seated curl, overhead triceps work, calf
  raises that work the stretched bottom half of the range, incline curls. This
  is low-risk and evidence-informed. WEAK for _replacing_ full range of motion
  with lengthened partials in trained lifters (few trials, mostly untrained).
  Full range with a controlled stretch is the default.

### 2.15 Reps at %1RM, e1RM formulas and their error

- Nuzzo 2024 [VF], 269 studies and 7,289 people: the mean and the
  between-person SD of reps to failure at each %1RM. The SD shrinks as load
  rises. Sex, age and training status show no clear effect. The bench press
  and leg press differ: the leg press allows more reps than the bench at
  every load from 50% to 95% of 1RM, and more than the squat at 65–90%. One
  table serves most exercises, with separate tables for those two. The
  tables are in the verified block at the end of this section.
- The classic textbook table (NSCA, quoted as Nuzzo's Table 1) is
  CONVENTION. Against Nuzzo's means it under-counts reps, by about 1 at 90%
  and about 3 at 70%. Plan with Nuzzo's means:

  | %1RM | Textbook (CONVENTION) | Nuzzo main | Nuzzo bench |
  | ---- | --------------------- | ---------- | ----------- |
  | 95%  | 2                     | 3.3        | 2.6         |
  | 90%  | 4                     | 4.9        | 4.1         |
  | 85%  | 6                     | 7.2        | 6.2         |
  | 80%  | 8                     | 9.8        | 8.8         |
  | 75%  | 10                    | 12.4       | 11.5        |
  | 70%  | 11                    | 14.8       | 14.1        |

  In the lab, each rep is worth about 2.5% of 1RM from 2 to 6 reps (2.4% on
  the main table, 2.7% on the bench) and about 2% from 6 to 10 [C]. The owner
  source's "about 4% load per rep dropped" is clearly steeper. From 1 to 6
  reps it drops 20 points, against 12.6 (main) to 14.5 (bench) for Nuzzo and
  16.7 for Epley [C]. Of the curves compared, it sits closest to Marzagao's
  curve from app logs at heavy loads.

- **e1RM formulas**: Epley multiplies the load by 1 + reps/30. It comes from
  a 1985 poundage chart with no published sample (as described in Marzagao
  2026 [VF]). Brzycki 1993 [U] multiplies by 36/(37 − reps). They agree
  exactly at 10 reps [C]. Adding reported reps in reserve to reps performed
  is the usual RPE-chart practice (CONVENTION).
- **Their error:**
  - Every equation tested under-predicted the deadlift 1RM, in 67 untrained
    college students (LeSuer 1997 [VA]). The size, 9–14%, is not in the
    abstract [U].
  - Accuracy falls with reps, so estimate from 10 reps or fewer (Reynolds
    2006 [VA]).
  - Marzagao 2026 [VF]: on 303,494 near-failure sets from Fitbod users,
    letting Epley's factor vary with the load cut inconsistency by 17–22%
    against Epley, Brzycki, Wathen and Mayhew. The gain ranged from about
    +1% for heavy barbell lifts to +40% for light dumbbell and cable work.
- **The noise floor.** 1RM test–retest reliability has a median CV of 4.2%,
  with a range of 0.5–12.1% (Grgic 2020 [VF]). It was 3.3% in lifters with
  training experience and 5.5% in the untrained. With familiarisation it was
  3.8%, without it 5.3%. It was 3.5% in young and middle-aged adults and
  5.4% in older adults.
- **Product implications.** MODERATE.
  - A first e1RM from one set of 3–8 reps to failure is good to about ±5–7%
    [C], because people differ in how many reps they get at a given load.
    Tracking one person on one lift is tighter, because their personal offset
    cancels. From 12 or more reps, the error is large.
  - On the deadlift, formulas run low. How far is unverified [U].
  - Any stall or PR logic must look past day-to-day noise of about 3.5% for
    trained lifters and about 5.5% for novices and older adults.

- Dropping from 6 reps to 4 adds about 6% (Nuzzo and Epley), not 8%. At 6 reps, the "≈83%" in OREB-45 is Epley's figure; Brzycki gives 86%.

**e1RM formulas and their error.**

- Epley: 1RM = load × (1 + reps/30). It comes from a 1985 poundage chart with no published sample (as described in Marzagao 2026).
- Brzycki: 1RM = load × 36/(37 − reps). Source: _JOPERD_ 64(1):88–90, doi:10.1080/07303084.1993.10606684.
- The two formulas agree exactly at 10 reps [C].
- On a true set to failure, Epley reads high against Nuzzo's means [C]: +2–4% on the bench (70–95%), +5–6% on other lifts (75–95%) and +14–17% on the leg press.
- Brzycki is within ±2.5% at 85–95%, but runs high beyond 10 reps [C].
- One between-person SD of reps moves an Epley estimate by ±4–6% on the bench and ±5–7% on other lifts [C]. A first estimate from one set is good to about ±5–7%, not ±3–5%.
- Tracking one person on one lift is tighter, because their personal offset cancels.
- LeSuer 1997 (_JSCR_ 11(4):211–213, doi:10.1519/00124278-199711000-00001) tested 7 equations on 67 untrained college students (40 men, 27 women).
  - Every correlation was above 0.95.
  - The mean error differed from zero for 5 of 7 equations on the bench and 6 of 7 on the squat.
  - Every equation underestimated the deadlift.
- Reynolds 2006 (_JSCR_ 20(3):584–592, doi:10.1519/R-15304.1, PMID 16937972) tested 70 adults aged 18–69 on the bench press and leg press.
  - A 5RM predicted the 1RM best: R² 0.993 on the bench, 0.974 on the leg press.
  - Use no more than 10 reps.
  - Bench: 1RM = 1.1307 × 5RM + 0.70 kg (SEE 2.98 kg). A bench 5RM is about 88% of 1RM.
- Marzagao 2026 (arXiv:2603.17495, preprint) took 303,494 sets from 14,966 users on 388 exercises, drawn from 37.7 million logged sets.
  - Formula: 1RM = w × (1 + (r − 1)^0.85 / (−2.55 + 4.58 ln w)).
  - Inconsistency fell by 17.0–21.9% against Wathen, Epley, Brzycki and Mayhew.
  - By exercise, the gain ran from +1% for heavy barbell lifts to +40% for light dumbbell and cable work.
  - It measures consistency, not accuracy against tested maxima. 80% of users were men.
- Grgic 2020 (_Sports Med Open_ 6:31, PMID 32681399): 32 studies and 1,595 people. ICC 0.64–0.99 (median 0.97). CV 0.5–12.1% (median 4.2%).

**For the virtual lifter (§4.4).**

- Draw a personal z-score once per lifter. Reps at a load = mean(load) + z × SD(load). A fixed offset in reps cannot reproduce an SD that falls from 4.4 reps at 60% to 1.7 at 95%.
- Effort reports come from Halperin 2022 (_Sports Med_ 52:377–390, PMID 34542869): 12 studies and 414 people.
  - People under-predict the reps they have left by 0.95 (95% CI 0.17–1.73).
  - The between-person SD of that error is 1.45 reps (0.99–2.12). Model it as a stable personal bias, not as noise from set to set.
  - The error grows by about 0.06 reps per rep up to 12 reps, and by 0.47 per rep beyond that.
  - Predictions improve nearer failure. Training status made no difference.

**The app's Epley use [C].**

- The rep-to-load scaling (`represcribe.ts`, `startingLoads.ts`) is within 1.5% of Nuzzo between 3 and 12 reps. For example, from 8 reps to 5 it multiplies the load by 1.086; Nuzzo gives 1.077–1.080. It can stay.
- `estimate1RMRange` shows ±5% for 2–6 reps, ±10% for 7–10 and ±20% for 11–15. These are about 1, 1.5–2 and 2.5–3 between-person SDs.
- On true sets to failure, those bands are centred 2–6% high, and about 15% high on the leg press.

**Still unverified.**

- How large LeSuer's deadlift underestimate is. The "9–14%" is not in the abstract, which gives only the direction.
- Reps at each %1RM for the deadlift and overhead press. No meta-analytic table exists.
- Set-to-set noise in one person's effort ratings.
- Marzagao's curve, checked against tested 1RMs.

### 2.16 Individual response

- Hubal 2005 [VF]: in 585 untrained people over 12 weeks, elbow-flexor 1RM
  changed by 0 to +250% (0 to +10.2 kg) and CSA by −2 to +59%.
- Ahtiainen 2016 [VF]: over 20–24 weeks, strength rose 21.1 ± 11.5%
  (range −8 to +60%) and size 4.8 ± 6.1% (range −11 to +30%).
- Hammarström 2020 [VA]: three sets beat one set (CSA +5.2% vs +3.7%), but
  only about 38% (size) and 47% (strength) of people clearly benefited from
  the higher volume.
- Dankel & Loenneke 2020 and Hecksteden 2015 [VA]: observed spread includes
  measurement error and biological noise, so true response variance is
  smaller than the raw ranges.
- A replicated within-participant trial (Robinson, Steele, Helms, Trexler et
  al., bioRxiv preprint, 2025) [VA] was designed to measure true individual
  volume response. Sixteen recreationally trained people trained one leg at
  about 8 sets a week and the other at about 16, in two 11-week phases with
  a 6–8 week break between. Higher volume gave more vastus lateralis CSA
  (+1.8 cm², 95% HDI 0.29 to 3.41) but no detectable strength advantage
  (+3.48 kg, HDI −5.1 to 12.15). It found no clear evidence of a meaningful individual
  response to volume. Volume-specific response correlations were 0.04–0.06,
  against 0.67–0.70 for a person's response to training in general. This
  argues against a per-person volume-response setting. The trial is small,
  and a failure to detect is not proof of absence.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. Never promise an outcome. Give
  ranges. People differ in how they respond to training in general, but
  there is no clear evidence that some respond specifically to more volume.
  Labelling one person a "responder" from one block is mostly noise.

### 2.17 Sex and age

- Nuzzo 2024 [VF]: sex, age and training status have no clear effect on reps
  at a given %1RM, so one reps–%1RM model serves everyone. Women's SD is
  about 20–25% larger at 60–75%.
- Roberts 2020 [VA]: similar relative hypertrophy and lower-body strength gains
  in women and men, and larger relative upper-body strength gains in women.
- Refalo 2025 [VA]: similar relative muscle growth, larger absolute growth in
  men.
- Jones 2021 [VA]: in adults over 50, men gained more in absolute terms.
  Women gained more relative lower-body strength (g −0.21, p = 0.0003).
  Relative upper-body strength and muscle size did not differ.
- Peterson 2010 [VF]: in 47 studies of 1,079 people aged 50–92, strength rose
  about 25–30% on average (24–33% by test). Heavier loads gained more: +5.3%
  for each step up in intensity.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE.
  - Program by the person's numbers, not their sex. Rates in percent can be
    shared, and absolute starting loads differ.
  - The repo's 0.75 female starting-load factor (LIFT-EV-07, fenced) is a
    cold-start convenience, not a programming rule.
  - These sources have no younger comparison group: Jones and Peterson
    studied only people over 50. They do not show that older lifters progress
    or grow less than younger ones, and Peterson found no link between age
    and strength gains within its sample. More conservative recovery for
    older lifters remains an assumption (WEAK).

### 2.18 Detraining, retraining and "muscle memory"

- Bosquet 2013 [VA]: strength losses grow with the length of the break. How
  small they are in the first weeks is not in the abstract [U].
- A trial in trained men [R] found no 1RM loss after two weeks off.
- Ogasawara 2013 [VF]: a three-week break after every six weeks of training
  gave the same 24-week bench and CSA gains as training throughout. Each
  retraining block gained at about the rate of the first 6 weeks, not
  faster. Continuous training had slowed after week 9.
- Seaborne 2018 [VF]: faster regrowth on retraining, linked to DNA
  methylation. Eight untrained men enrolled and one withdrew, so the lean-mass
  and reloading data are n = 7.
- Psilander 2019 [VF]: 19 people trained one leg for 10 weeks. After 20 weeks
  off, size was back to baseline and about 60% of the strength gain was kept.
  In 5 weeks of retraining, the trained leg passed its old best (45 to 49 kg,
  against a 48 kg peak). It gained no faster than in the first block, or
  than the untrained leg (41 to 47 kg). Muscle thickness rose about 10% in
  the 5 weeks, as much as in the 10-week first block, but just as much in the
  untrained leg. The authors suggest swelling, not memory. There was no
  myonuclear "memory" signal.
- Coleman 2024 [VF]: one week off mid-programme left lower-body strength
  gains a little smaller than training straight through. There was no
  absolute loss.
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE–WEAK.
  - Short breaks cost little. In Ogasawara 2013, each 3-week break cost
    2.0–3.3% of bench 1RM and 2.6–2.9% of triceps CSA. How little two weeks
    off costs is unchecked [U]: Bosquet 2013's abstract gives no duration
    bands, and the trial in trained men is unidentified. Secondary accounts
    suggest little loss through about 2 weeks, and measurable loss by about 3.
  - Retraining is not faster than first training (Ogasawara 2013; Psilander
    2019). What returns quickly is the part of the gain that was kept, so a
    returning lifter passes their old best sooner than they first reached it.
  - Lift4's Welcome back is conservative and reasonable: 10% lighter after 3–8
    weeks away, 20% after longer, and climbing back a step a session.

### 2.19 Concurrent training (lifting and running)

**What the meta-analyses show.**

- Wilson 2012 [VA]: interference grew with endurance frequency (r −0.26 to
  −0.35) and duration (r −0.29 to −0.75). Running, but not cycling,
  significantly reduced hypertrophy and strength, and power suffered most.
  Effect sizes, lift-only vs concurrent: hypertrophy 1.23 vs 0.85, strength
  1.76 vs 1.44, power 0.91 vs 0.55. Later meta-analyses do not confirm these
  moderators: Schumann 2022 found neither frequency nor modality a moderator.
  WEAK–MODERATE for the moderators.
- Schumann 2022 [VF]: on average, no interference for hypertrophy or maximal
  strength. Explosive strength is blunted, more when both are done in the
  same session.
- Huiberts 2024 [VF]: lower-body strength is blunted in men (−0.43) but not
  in women (0.08).
- Petré 2021 [VF]: lower-body 1RM is blunted only in trained people.
- Lundberg 2022 [VF]: a small negative effect on fibre hypertrophy, perhaps
  larger with running.

**Order and spacing.**

- Within one session, lift before running when lower-body strength is the
  priority (Murlasits 2018 [VA]; Eddens 2018 [VF]).
- Separate conflicting sessions by at least about 6 hours (Robineau 2016
  [VA]).
- Leg strength work raises the cost of running the next day. At 6 hours the
  results are mixed: economy was unchanged in one study and worse in another,
  and time to exhaustion fell after heavy sessions (Doma & Deakin 2013–14
  [VA]). After one heavy leg session in lifting-naive men, the cost of
  running rose from 1.13 to 1.19 kcal/kg/km at 24 hours and stayed raised for
  up to 48 hours (Doma 2019 [VF]).

**Benefit to running.** Strength training (heavy, explosive or plyometric)
improved running economy by 2–8% in 14 of the 20 trials that measured it,
over 6–20 weeks. Time trials (1.5–10 km) also tended to improve, though not
in every study (Blagrove 2018 [VF]). In Eihara 2022 [VF], heavy training
improved economy (g −0.32) and plyometrics did not detectably (−0.13). For
time trials, plyometrics helped (−0.17), and heavy training's −0.24 was not
significant. Across trials, heavy load improved time trial and time to
exhaustion (ES −0.469, Llanos-Lagos 2024b [VF]). High-load gains in economy
showed above 12 km/h and in highly trained runners (Llanos-Lagos 2024a
[VA]).

**Grade and implications.** MODERATE.

- For the typical Tropos runner who lifts, the cost falls on lower-body
  maximal and explosive strength, mostly in men and trained lifters. It is
  larger when lifting and running share a session, and may grow with running
  volume.
- Upper-body strength and hypertrophy are barely touched.
- Keep loads heavy, trim leg volume when mileage builds, and keep heavy legs
  away from the day before key runs. Lift4 does all three.

### 2.20 Progression method

- Plotkin 2022 [VF]: adding reps and adding load produced similar hypertrophy
  and strength over 8 weeks in trained lifters. Strength slightly favoured
  adding load (2.0 kg, 90% CI −2.4 to 7.8). No arm tested double
  progression: each lever worked alone, so the trial supports double
  progression only indirectly. WEAK–MODERATE.
- Progressive _set_ increases within a mesocycle (the RP pattern) have one
  trial behind them. Enes 2024 [VA] started 31 trained men at 22
  quadriceps sets a week, then held volume constant or added 4 or 6 sets
  every two weeks, for 12 weeks. Squat 1RM gains rose with the added sets.
  Size did not differ between groups (details in §2.22). Enes 2025 [VA], in
  trained women, agrees for strength. WEAK: one lab, legs only.

### 2.21 Nutrition as a moderator

- Energy deficits blunt lean-mass gains but not strength gains (Murphy &
  Koehler 2022 [VF]).
- Protein's benefit to lean mass plateaus near 1.6 g/kg/day (95% CI
  1.03–2.20). The authors suggest about 2.2 g/kg/day to be safe. No
  breakpoint was estimated for strength (Morton 2018 [VF]).
- **Grade and implications.** MODERATE. The simulation needs an energy-balance
  multiplier on hypertrophy, and the app should not promise muscle gain to
  someone on Lose fat.

#### Verified parameters (2026-10-07)

Checked against primary sources on 2026-10-07. Markers are per source: [VF] full text read, [VA] abstract only.

**ACSM 2026** [VF]. Currier BS … Phillips SM, _MSSE_ 58(4):851–72, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897 (open access: PMC12965823). It reviews 137 systematic reviews, more than 30,000 people, searched to October 2024. It says:

- The main recommendation is high-effort resistance training at least twice a week, with all major muscle groups engaged. It does not say each muscle twice a week. With volume equated, 1 vs more than 5 days a week made no difference to growth.
- Strength: ≥80% of 1RM, full range of motion, 2–3 sets ("2–3 sets/session", Table 6), early in the session, at least 2 sessions a week.
- Size: ≥10 sets per muscle group per week, with more helping, and eccentric overload.
- Not consistently important: training to failure, equipment type, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood-flow restriction and periodization. For strength, the effect of periodization "could not be determined".
- Reading Pelland 2025, it puts diminishing returns past about 2–3 sets per exercise for strength and about 18–20 weekly sets for size. It advises at least two sets per exercise.
- Counting unit, in the authors' view: four regions (upper and lower body, push and pull) cover the major muscles. Six if upper-body push and pull are split into horizontal and vertical. The requirement can be met per session or per week.

It matches Lift4's defaults closely. Its frequency line is a floor for the whole programme, not proof that each muscle needs two sessions.

**Adding sets over time.**

- Enes 2024 [VA], _MSSE_ 56(3):553–63, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003317. 31 trained men (5.1 ± 2.2 years of training; squat 1.7 × body mass), legs twice a week for 12 weeks. All started at 22 quadriceps sets a week. One group stayed at 22, one added 4 sets every two weeks (to 42), one added 6 (to 52). Squat 1RM: +6 beat +4 (P = 0.002), and +4 beat constant (P = 0.023). Quadriceps size did not differ between groups (P = 0.067 and 0.076). The set counts and effort (about 2 RIR, last set of each exercise to failure) are from the same cohort's re-analysis, _J Hum Kinet_ 2026, doi:10.5114/jhk/209051 [VF].
- That re-analysis: session strain rose with volume (+6 > +4 > constant). Sessions felt less pleasant at +6 than at constant volume. Six dropped out, four for loss of interest.
- Enes 2025 [VA], _J Sports Sci_, doi:10.1080/02640414.2025.2459003. 30 trained women, 12 weeks, constant 22 sets vs +2 or +4 a fortnight. Both progressions beat constant for leg-press 1RM. +4 beat constant for vastus lateralis CSA. Muscle thickness did not differ.
- Limits: legs only, 12 weeks, one lab, a high starting volume. This supports adding sets as a lever when strength stalls. It does not show that rising volume is needed for size.

**The upper limit.** Räntilä & Ahtiainen 2026 [VF], _J Sci Sport Exerc_, doi:10.1007/s42978-026-00387-7 (open copy: https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/91cae5f5-7294-4d9b-b963-73609dfa1c89/download).

- 32 national-level male athletes (track and field, floorball; at least 3 years of resistance training).
- Ten shared weeks at 32–41 weekly sets: vastus lateralis +7.0% ± 9.8%, back squat +19.1%, deadlift +12.5%.
- Then 7 weeks at normal volume (n = 12) or about 1.5 × (n = 15; "targeting 40 vs. 60 sets", up to 63 weekly sets per muscle group).
- The extra volume added no size or strength. Deadlift favoured the normal group (P = 0.043). It did not rescue non-responders. Five of 32 withdrew with musculoskeletal problems.
- Limits: small; sport practice not controlled; ultrasound at one site; diet not controlled.
- Product: no case for weekly volumes far past 20–30 sets per muscle. Lift4's ceilings stand.

## 3. Programming by goal and training age

**Training age, defined operationally** (after Rippetoe & Baker [VA];
CONVENTION, detectable from data):

| Level        | Definition                                                                                  |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Novice       | Still adds load session to session. Often the first 3–9 months of consistent training.      |
| Intermediate | Progress is weekly at best. The stage can end after about 2–4 or more years.                |
| Advanced     | Progress shows over a month or a block, with near-ceiling 1RMs. The book gives no duration. |

The durations are the book's rough guides. It defines the level by how fast
someone recovers and progresses, and says that neither the weight lifted nor
years of training classify a trainee.

A data-driven level is the e1RM slope divided by its noise over 6–8 weeks.
That is more honest than self-report, which skews toward the middle.

**The rows below hold for every goal unless a goal says otherwise.**

- Train at least twice a week, working every major muscle group (ACSM 2026
  [VF]). Twice a week per muscle is a way to fit the weekly sets, not a floor
  of its own: with volume equated, frequency made no clear difference to
  growth (Schoenfeld 2019 [VA]). MODERATE.
- Main lifts first in the session (STRONG for strength).
- Rest: 2–3 minutes on heavy compounds and at least 60–90 seconds on
  isolations (MODERATE).
- The progression unit is the lift instance, judged on all working sets
  (CONVENTION).

### 3.3 Powerbuilding

The combination is CONVENTION built from MODERATE parts.

- **Main lifts:** strength structure, twice a week each. A top set of 3–6
  reps at RPE 7–9, or heavy fixed sets, then back-offs of 6–10.
- **Accessories:** hypertrophy structure. 8–20 reps near failure, 10–20
  fractional sets per muscle a week.
- **Novice:** Build muscle with fixed 5s on the main lifts is already a
  powerbuilding programme (Lift4's beginner rows do this).
- **Intermediate:** heavier and lighter days, which is the DUP-lite Lift4
  already has.
- **Advanced:** alternate strength-emphasis and hypertrophy-emphasis blocks
  of 4–8 weeks.
- **Evidence:** combining heavy work with volume serves both outcomes
  (Currier 2023 [VF]: heavy loads, multiple sets and three sessions a week
  ranked top for strength; for size every prescription did about as well,
  with heavy multiple sets twice a week ranked top).

### 3.4 General fitness

ACSM 2009 [VF] and ACSM 2026 [VF]. MODERATE: the two stands agree on the
outline and differ on the numbers.

- Two or three full-body sessions a week (ACSM 2009, for novices). ACSM 2026
  asks for training at least twice a week with every major muscle group
  worked. It notes that the 2009 full-body advice rested on three studies
  (n = 59), two of which did not test frequency.
- At least two sets per exercise: ACSM 2026 finds two sets beat one. ACSM
  2009 starts novices on 1–3 sets.
- 8–12 reps for novices (ACSM 2009). ACSM 2026 sets no 8–15 range; the
  8–20 reps it quotes summarise earlier guidelines.
- Effort near failure, or about 2–3 reps in reserve (ACSM 2026, which finds
  too little evidence for exact targets). ACSM 2009 sets no reps-in-reserve
  target.
- Double progression: add 2–10% load once the target reps are beaten by 1–2
  on two sessions in a row (ACSM 2009).
- Cover the movement patterns. Any equipment works (in ACSM 2026, equipment
  type did not consistently change outcomes), and any training beats none.
  The minimal dose (one set per exercise) still works when time is short;
  ACSM 2026 accepts it when individualising.
- Lighter weeks are optional.

### 3.5 Lifting to support running

MODERATE.

- **Sessions:** two a week in the base phase, one or two in build and peak.
- **Loads:** heavy, 3–6 reps at ≥80% or fixed 5s, for running economy.
- **Volume:**
  - leg volume kept low (4–8 hard sets a week) and trimmed as mileage
    builds;
  - upper body as time allows.
- **Placement:**
  - lift on hard-run days, lifting first or at least 6 hours after the run;
  - avoid heavy legs within about 24–48 hours of a key run;
  - the taper drops lifting volume by about half for the last two weeks.
- **Plyometrics and explosive work** may add economy benefit. That belongs to
  the running workstream.

Two cautions apply to every short trial. Gains in "trained" RCT samples often
include test practice and a novelty boost from a new, specific, supervised
programme. Gains are also front-loaded after any programme change. A
simulator calibrated only to 8-week trained RCTs will overstate long-run
intermediate progress. Calibrating only to multi-year cohorts will understate
short-term gains after a programme change.

#### Verified parameters (2026-10-07)

Checked against PubMed and Europe PMC abstracts. Full text was read where open: Latella 2020 (PMC7448836), Helms 2018 (PMC5877330), the SportRxiv preprints of Steele 2023 and Latella 2024, and the ACSM 2009 stand. [C] marks a value computed here from a paper's own model or table.

**Untrained lifters**

- Steele 2023 (PMID 35591809): 14,690 gym members, 60% women, aged 48 ± 11. One set to failure on six machines, once a week, for up to 352 weeks. Gains were about 30–50% in year 1 and 50–60% by about 6 years. Progress was practically flat after 1–2 years. "Strength" here is the training load for 4–6 slow reps, not a 1RM.
- Steele's fitted curves [C]:
  - chest press +18% at 8 weeks, +25% at 16, +29% at 26 and +35% at 52;
  - leg press +24%, +32%, +38% and +46%;
  - year 2 adds only about 5% more.
- Ahtiainen 2016 (PMID 26767377): 287 untrained people aged 19–78. Strength +21.1 ± 11.5% (−8 to +60%). Size +4.8 ± 6.1% (−11 to +30%). For strength, 12% were high responders and 7% low responders.
- Hubal 2005 (PMID 15947721): 585 people, 12 weeks, one arm. Elbow-flexor 1RM rose 54% on average (range 0 to +250%). Biceps CSA rose 19% (range −2 to +59%). Women gained more relative strength. This is a small single-joint lift, not a guide to the bench.
- Hammarström 2020 (PMID 31813190): leg CSA +5.2% with 3 sets and +3.7% with 1 set, in 12 weeks.
- ACSM figures (~40% untrained, 20% moderately trained, 16% trained, 10% advanced, 2% elite, over 4 weeks to 2 years):
  - they come from the 2002 stand (Kraemer et al., PMID 11828249), not the 2009 one;
  - the 2009 full text does not contain them;
  - they are totals over mixed study lengths, not yearly rates.

**Trained lifters**

- Latella 2024 (PMID 38060089): OpenPowerlifting, IPF raw, 9,259 lifters with 3 or more meets, up to about 17 years. Gains were +7.5–12.5% in the first year of competing and about 12.5–20% after 10 years, on a linear-log fit.
- That curve implies [C]:
  - year 2 adds about 18% of the year-1 gain, and year 3 about 10%;
  - in practice, about +1.3–2.2% in year 2 and +0.8–1.3% in year 3.
- Steele 2023, an exploratory fit on 10,000 OpenPowerlifting lifters: about +12.5% over about 6 years, flat after about 1 year of competing.
- Latella 2020 (PMID 32865942): 1,897 Powerlifting Australia lifters.
  - The 15 years is the dataset window. Mean follow-up was 1.6–1.8 years.
  - Men's totals rose 0.15 kg a day. Extrapolated, that is about 55 kg, or 10.7%, a year.
  - Women's rose 0.12 kg a day, about 44 kg or 15% a year [C].
  - The strongest quarter of men gained half as fast as the weakest: 0.10 against 0.21 kg a day.
  - Gains are best minus first total, so they are biased upwards.
- Latella 2022 (PMID 35019902), same method: bench +10.5–12.8 kg a year, squat +20.2–25.4 kg and deadlift +18.1–21.1 kg.
- Solberg 2019 (PMID 30958059): world-class powerlifters improved 12% (SD 10%) over the 5 years before their peak age, about 2.3% a year [C].
- Helms 2018 (PMID 29628895): 21 men with 2 or more years of training. Their bench was 117 ± 19 kg, about 1.47× bodyweight. Over 8 weeks of benching 3 times a week, ending in a taper:
  - bench +9.6 ± 5.4 kg and +10.7 ± 3.3 kg (+8.5–8.9%);
  - squat +13.9 kg and +17.1 kg (+10–12%);
  - muscle thickness +6–10% [C];
  - chance of a bench gain of 10% or more: about 36% [C].
- Androulakis-Korakakis 2020 (PMID 31797219): in trained men, one hard set 2–3 times a week still raised the bench by 8.3 kg (95% CI 0.7–15.8) and the squat by 17.5 kg.

**Sex**

- Roberts 2020 (PMID 32218059): women gained more relative upper-body strength (effect size −0.60). Lower-body strength (−0.21) and hypertrophy (0.07) did not differ.
- Refalo 2025 (PMID 40028215): relative muscle growth differs by +0.69% (−1.50 to +2.88%).

**Ceilings**

- van den Hoek 2024 (PMID 39060209): 809,986 tested raw entries. The 90th percentile at 18–35 years, in multiples of bodyweight:
  - squat 2.83 for men and 2.26 for women;
  - bench 1.95 and 1.35;
  - deadlift 3.25 and 2.66.

### 4.4 The virtual lifter: a response model to build and calibrate

Each component carries a grade. The model is deliberately modular, so the
run can swap one assumption and report the outcome _ranges_ across variants.
The response model encodes assumptions, so its outputs are not evidence that
one programme is better.

**State per lifter:**

- for each lift, base strength **B**, a heavy-load skill term **S** and a
  ceiling **B\***;
- for each muscle, a size index **M** (1 at the start) and a ceiling **M\***;
- acute fatigue **F** for the upper and lower body, and a slow
  accumulated-fatigue term;
- training age, and a detraining clock;
- fixed responder multipliers: r_s (strength), r_h (hypertrophy) and r_v
  (volume responsiveness).

**Weekly hypertrophy.**

**Skill and expression.** True 1RM = B · (1 + S).

- S rises toward S_max (about 0.05–0.08) with a time constant of 2–4 weeks
  when heavy specific work is present: sets of 5 reps or fewer at ≥85%.
- It decays toward a floor with a time constant of 4–8 weeks when that work
  is absent.
- It is meant to reproduce Mattocks 2017 [VA] (whether these magnitudes do is
  unchecked [U]), the front-loading of RCT gains, and the benefit of peaking. Direction MODERATE; magnitudes WEAK.

**Fatigue and day-to-day performance.**

- Performance P(t) = 1RM · (1 − F) · e^ε, with ε ~ N(0, σ_day²).
- σ_day ≈ 0.025–0.033 for trained lifters and 0.035–0.05 for novices. Grgic
  2020 [VF] gives median test–retest CVs of 3.3% for people with training
  experience and 5.5% for the untrained. A test–retest CV holds technical
  error and learning on top of day-to-day variation, so this doc reads those
  medians as rough ceilings; the review does not separate the parts. They are
  medians, not strict bounds: single studies run to 7.8% trained and 12.0%
  untrained.
- F decays with a time constant of 2–4 days after each session, scaled by the
  session's hard sets and closeness to failure. This is an assumption: no
  fitted source gives 2–4 days, and the strength-sport fits reported for
  Busso 1990 and 1994 put the fatigue time constant at 13–22 days [U].
- A slow component (time constant 10–14 days) accumulates when weekly load
  exceeds tolerance and dissipates in a lighter week.
- This is a Banister-family structure. As a synthesis of published fits: a
  fitness time constant of about 30–60 days if a fitness term is used; a
  fatigue time constant of about 5–15 days, sometimes lower or higher (about
  2–20); and a fatigue gain of a median of about 2× the fitness gain (IQR
  1.4–4 [C], across the published fits in which it exceeds the fitness
  gain), below 1 in some untrained, low-load fits (Busso 2003 [VF]).
  Calvert 1976 [VA] fitted one swimmer at 50 and 15 days. The 2× gain is
  Banister 1975's convention [U]. Use the structure only for short-term
  dynamics such as the deload rebound or a taper of +1–3%. Individual
  parameters are ill-conditioned, with wide confidence intervals, though the
  fitting itself was stable (Hellard 2006 [VF]; Stephens Hemingway 2020
  [VA]). WEAK.

**Reps achieved and effort reported.**

- Maximum reps at a load = Nuzzo's mean for that exercise class at
  (load ÷ today's P), plus z × Nuzzo's between-person SD at that load, with z
  drawn once per lifter (Nuzzo 2024 [VF]). A fixed offset in reps cannot
  work, because the SD falls as the load rises: 4.36 reps at 60%, 2.51 at 80%
  and 1.66 at 95%. A fixed percentage offset is not exact either: the SD runs
  at 0.22 of the mean at 60%, 0.26 at 80% and 0.51 at 95%. Nuzzo's classes
  are main lifts, bench press and leg press. The authors say the SDs may
  partly be measurement error, so they may overstate the true spread between
  people.
- The lifter stops at the target or at failure.
- Reported reps in reserve = true reps in reserve + a personal bias drawn once
  per lifter, with mean −0.95 reps and SD 1.45 (Halperin 2022 [VF]: 12
  studies, 414 people). Set-to-set noise is not quantified. Errors grow past
  12 reps (0.47 reps per extra rep, against 0.06 below) and further from
  failure; training status made no difference. MODERATE.

**Responders.**

- r_s ~ lognormal(0, 0.35) and r_h ~ lognormal(0, 0.45), correlated about
  0.3.
- In Hammarström 2020 [VA], 13 of 34 untrained adults (38%) gained clearly
  more muscle from 3 sets than from 1, and 16 of 34 (47%) clearly more
  strength. These are observed counts, not noise-corrected responder rates.
- To remove measurement noise, Dankel & Loenneke 2020 [VA] take random error
  from each study's own time-matched control group, so the correction depends
  on the dataset: multiply an observed SD by √(1 − SD_control²/SD_training²)
  from a named dataset. No flat 30% shrink could be found in Dankel &
  Loenneke [U]; if one is used, it is this doc's own assumption (WEAK).
- WEAK–MODERATE.

**Detraining and retraining.**

- Short breaks cost a little. In Ogasawara 2013 [VF], each 3-week break
  lowered bench 1RM by 2.0–3.3% and triceps CSA by 2.6–2.9%, about a quarter
  of the first block's size gain. "No loss over the first 2–3 weeks" is
  unchecked: Bosquet 2013's abstract gives no duration bands [U].
- Longer breaks: in Psilander 2019 [VF], strength lost about 40% of its gain
  over 20 weeks off, about 1.9% of the gain a week. A decline of 1–2% of the
  gain a week is consistent with that; Bosquet 2013's rates are unchecked
  [U].
- The trials do not show size falling more slowly early on. Muscle thickness
  lost all its gain over 20 weeks in Psilander 2019, though fibre size held.
  Leg lean mass lost about 72% of its gain in 7 weeks in Seaborne 2018 [VF]
  (untrained men, n = 7).
- On return, the trials disagree. Ogasawara 2013 and Psilander 2019 found
  retraining no faster than first training (about ×1.0). Seaborne 2018 found
  about ×1.6 for lean mass and ×1.8 for torque, and the faster rate ran past
  the previous best. Sweep the multiplier from ×1.0 to about ×1.6–1.8; do not
  stop it at the previous best.
- WEAK.

**Acute effects of running.**

- Leg sessions within 24 hours of a long or hard run perform at ×0.93–0.97.
  This is an assumption (WEAK): no source checked measured it, and Doma 2013
  [VA] found knee-extensor torque was not significantly reduced by a run. The
  Doma studies' impairment runs the other way: heavy leg work cut running time
  to exhaustion 6 hours later.

**Calibration.** Fit k_s and k_h so that a novice at the reference dose hits
the §4.2 and §4.3 central values, then check:

- the shape against Latella 2024 and Steele 2023, and trained lifters'
  rates against Latella 2020;
- the 8-week trained RCT gains (Helms 2018) as an upper tail after a
  programme change; that trial implies about a 36% chance of a bench gain of
  10% or more in 8 weeks [C];
- Ahtiainen 2016's spread;
- that Lift4's own outputs reproduce sensible miss rates.

### 4.6 Reconciling the owner's coaching anchors

`owner-lifting-sources.md` records Oreb's timelines for coached men of
80–100 kg: a 100 kg bench inside year 1, 140 kg in about two years, and a
double-bodyweight squat in one to two years. Against §4.2:

- **100 kg inside a year from an untrained 60 kg bench (+67%).** Near the top
  decile (central +40%, 80% upper bound +60%). Plausible for coached high
  responders gaining bodyweight. Not the median.
- **140 kg in two years (+133% from 60 kg).** Beyond the two-year 80% interval
  (a central of roughly +55–65%, an extrapolation; Steele's minimal-dose
  cohort was nearly flat after 1–2 years). It suits lifters who start
  stronger or heavier, or the top few percent.
- **A double-bodyweight squat (160–180 kg) in one to two years from a 70–80 kg
  start (+110–150%).** The upper tail, plausible in two years for some.

Use these as checks that the simulator's 90th–95th percentiles reach them,
not as pass/fail medians. That is how the owner-sources file itself framed
them.

## 5. Adaptive lifting apps

### 5.1 How the apps describe their models

**Verified parameters (2026-10-07).** [VA]: read directly on the vendor's help
centre, official blog, App Store listing or shipped app copy on 2026-10-07.
These are the vendors' own descriptions. None is a validation. URLs are in
`explanation-ux.md` §3.

- **RP Hypertrophy (RP Strength; web app, US iOS app since 20 December 2025)** [VA].
  - Blocks of up to 8 weeks. "the last week of the block is always a deload" (https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/43864718052887).
  - Load rises "a few percentage points each week". When the next weight is too big a jump, "it adds a rep to each set instead" (https://help.rpstrength.com/hc/en-us/articles/43864744488599).
  - Sets per muscle go up, stay or go down from four ratings (shipped copy, https://training.rpstrength.com/assets/index-6f9ec378.js):
    - joint pain per exercise;
    - pump per muscle;
    - volume ("number of hard sets") per muscle;
    - soreness per muscle, asked at the next session about the last one.
  - The setup copy says the app pushes "closer to failure" across the block.
  - The best-known feedback-driven volume model. No published validation of its rules was found.
- **JuggernautAI (Juggernaut Training Systems, 2021)** [VA].
  - Powerlifting and powerbuilding.
  - A questionnaire sets individual MEV and MRV. A pre-session readiness check (sleep, mood, energy, soreness) adjusts the day.
  - Top-set and back-off RPEs adjust the next sets in real time. Check-ins at the end of each session, week, block and programme adjust later training (https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/3-how-juggernautai-is-individualized-to-you).
  - Final phases and tapers are built around the meet date (https://help.jtsstrength.com/en/articles/17-training-phases).
  - Readiness score: "around 0 to ~30" in the help centre; 0–100 from v3.0. Low readiness drops accessory sets (https://www.juggernautai.app/blog/juggernautai-25).
  - The closest commercial analogue to a "meet mode".
- **MacroFactor Workouts (Stronger By Science Technologies; launched January 2026)** [VA].
  - "Rule-based logic, not generative AI" (https://macrofactor.com/workouts/).
  - Each set has a rep range and an RIR target. Expected max is the middle of the range plus the RIR target (7–9 at 2 RIR predicts 10). Beating it raises load or reps; falling short "may recommend slightly less weight" (https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/372-what-does-progressive-overload-mean-in-macrofactor-workouts).
  - RIR 0–6+ is optional.
  - Deloads are optional, on the first or last cycle. Programmes run on cycles, not dates. The structure never changes by itself; only targets update after each workout.
- **Fitbod** [VA].
  - Builds each workout from goal, level, equipment and split, plus per-muscle recovery (0–100%) and Estimated Strength ("effectively a projected one-rep max").
  - "mStrength" deliberately alternates heavier and lighter sessions.
  - Max Effort Days (two AMRAP sets) recalibrate the estimate.
  - RiR 0–4+ is optional (https://help.fitbod.me/hc/en-us/articles/43489869175063).
  - The 2026 Marzagao preprint [VF] fitted its e1RM formula to Fitbod logs: a 37.7-million-set extract (a 5% sample of users), of which 303,494 near-failure sets were analysed. It does not describe how Fitbod builds its recommendations.
- **Alpha Progression** [VA].
  - Per-set recommendations from an "estimated 10RM", the reference workout, today's sets, the planned rep target and available weights. Within a session, the next set's recommendation updates after each logged set.
  - RIR is optional (inferred when off) (https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/alpha-progression-progression-recommendations).
  - Plans default to 4 weeks. RIR, periodisation and deloads are recommended off for beginners (https://alphaprogression.com/en/blog/alpha-progression-workout-plan-generator).
  - Volume charts. Per-muscle targets were not confirmed on its own pages.
- **Gravl** [VA].
  - Starting 1RMs are estimated from sex, bodyweight and experience.
  - Weights adjust from the last session, a three-value exertion question asked after each exercise, and exercise order (https://gravl.ai/help/gravl-science-understand-your-recommended-weights).
  - Per-muscle recovery, with "Rest recommended" below 60%.
  - Deloads are user-started and don't update recommended weights.
- **Boostcamp** [VA].
  - A programme library (11,000+) whose authors' rules the app automates: "Hit your reps and the app increases the load next session" (https://www.boostcamp.app/).
  - Pro adds a questionnaire-built starter plan.
- **Liftosaur** [VA].
  - The model is the user's script: lp, dp, sum-of-reps or custom rules with state variables.
  - Rules run at the end of the workout and rewrite the programme text (https://www.liftosaur.com/features/progressions).
- **Contrast.**
  - Hevy's logger has templates and no model. Hevy Trainer (Pro) is algorithmic: "Automatic weight adjustments based on your performance" and stall reminders (https://www.hevyapp.com/features/workout-plan-generator/).
  - Strong was not re-checked.
  - Grade for every app above: vendor description, CONVENTION at best. None publishes a validation against a fixed programme (consistent with §5.3).

### 5.2 What a state-of-the-art adaptive model would include

Grades are on the evidence for each component, not for the whole.

1. **Per-lift e1RM with uncertainty.**
   - Update it from every working set: load, reps and reported reps in
     reserve.
   - Weight sets near 5 reps highest and discount sets above 10 (Reynolds
     2006: the 5RM predicted best, and 10 reps or fewer are advised).
     Widening the top band to 3–8 reps is CONVENTION.
   - Correct the deadlift, where formulas run low (LeSuer 1997 [VA]; by how
     much is unchecked [U]), and use a load-dependent formula (Marzagao 2026).
   - This drives stall detection, expectation bands and starting loads after
     swaps and returns.
   - MODERATE for the inputs, CONVENTION for Bayesian updating.
2. **A personal reps–%1RM curve.** Nuzzo 2024's means as a prior, updated
   from the person's own sets. MODERATE.
3. **Load selection from target reps and reps in reserve, for intermediate and
   advanced users who rate effort.** Helms 2018; Graham & Cleather 2019;
   Hickmott 2022. MODERATE.
4. **Fractional volume per muscle, spread across sessions.** Goal-specific
   weekly targets that allocate sets with diminishing returns in mind (Pelland
   2025). For growth, past about 11 fractional sets per muscle in one session,
   extra sets add less than can be detected, though growth keeps rising
   slowly; that is a detection point, not a cap (Remmert 2025). MODERATE.
5. **Fatigue-aware volume.** Add sets only while the e1RM trend and recovery
   reports allow, and reduce them when the trend falls beyond noise.
   CONVENTION or WEAK: RP-style feedback rules are not validated.
6. **Blocks with peaks, for strength users.** Accumulation, intensification
   and realisation, then a taper: volume cut about 30–50% (up to 70%) over
   1–2 weeks with intensity kept, then 2–7 days off (Travis 2020; Pritchard
   2015). MODERATE that periodization helps strength (Williams 2017;
   Moesgaard 2022). WEAK for specific block designs (Travis 2020; Pritchard
   2016).
7. **Priority muscles and lifts, chosen by the user.** Extra volume for one to
   three targets, with maintenance doses elsewhere (Spiering 2021;
   Androulakis-Korakakis 2020). MODERATE.
8. **Awareness of concurrent running.**
   - Session placement: keep hard leg work and running apart. Explosive
     strength suffers within one session and not with 3 h or more between
     (Schumann 2022; Robineau 2016).
   - Expectations: lower-body strength is blunted in men, not women (Huiberts
     2024), and explosive strength is blunted (Schumann 2022).
   - Leg volume scaled to running load: Wilson 2012 found interference grows
     with endurance frequency and duration, but Schumann 2022 found no such
     moderation.
   - MODERATE for placement and expectations, WEAK–MODERATE for scaling to
     running load.
9. **Stall detection against noise.** Judge an e1RM slope over 3–6 exposures
   against a σ of about 3–5.5% (median CV 4.2%; 3.3% for trained lifters, 5.5%
   untrained; Grgic 2020), not one or two sessions. MODERATE for the noise
   data, CONVENTION for the rule.
10. **Expectation bands.** Projections from training age and distance to the
    ceiling (§4). WEAK.

### 5.3 Where the evidence runs out

- **Personal volume landmarks.** No validated measure of an individual's
  MEV, MAV or MRV exists. The apps' feedback loops are plausible but untested.
- **Readiness and wellness questionnaires** have weak support as automatic
  load adjusters for lifting. HRV and readiness were not researched here.
- **Individual response prediction** is unreliable. Observed spread is
  inflated by noise.
- **Fitness–fatigue models** do not identify individual parameters.
- **No trial compares an adaptive app with a sound fixed programme.**
- **Long horizons are missing.** There are almost no RCTs longer than six
  months.
- **Women, older adults and advanced lifters** are underrepresented.

## 7. Rules not to adopt as defaults

Each is tempting, and the evidence does not support it as a universal default.

1. **Training to failure on every set, or on barbell compounds.** It adds
   little growth and no strength. Robinson 2024 puts failure against 2 reps
   in reserve at about 1 percentage point more muscle size (+0.04 SMD), with
   strength gains similar across a wide range of reps in reserve (Grgic 2022;
   Refalo 2023; Robinson 2024; ACSM 2026). Acute studies link failure to more
   fatigue; Robinson 2024 did not measure it.
2. **More sets is always better, or a high default** (20–30 sets per muscle).
   Returns diminish, extra sets in one session add less and less (Remmert
   2025's per-session figures are detection points, not caps), the time cost
   is high, and individuals vary. Strength gains little that is detectable
   past about 3–4 fractional sets a week: 30 weekly sets against 10 add 0.86
   percentage points of strength gain, below the smallest detectable effect
   (Pelland 2025).
3. **One set is enough for everyone.** The minimal dose works but is not
   optimal (Krieger 2010; Androulakis-Korakakis 2020).
4. **A fixed 3:1 or 4:1 deload cadence presented as physiology, or a deload
   that means a week off.** No trial supports any cadence, and a week fully
   off cost a little strength gain (Coleman 2024). Tropos's every-4th is a
   policy and should be described as one.
5. **Mandatory periodization models (block, DUP) for hypertrophy.**
   Periodization in general brought no hypertrophy benefit (Moesgaard 2022
   pooled every model: ES 0.13, not significant), and DUP matched linear
   (Grgic 2017). Block was not meta-analysed on its own, and its trials show
   no edge.
6. **Rep zones as switches.** "8–12 for size, 15+ to tone" is not supported:
   hypertrophy is load-independent when sets go to failure, shown down to
   about 30% of 1RM (Schoenfeld 2017). At light loads, stopping short costs
   growth (Schoenfeld 2021).
7. **Frequent rotation or "muscle confusion".** Excess variation does not
   seem to help and may hinder gains in size and strength, and a lift you
   want strong should be trained with priority and kept in regular rotation
   (Kassiano 2022). It also makes sessions hard to compare, which is a
   product reason, not a finding.
8. **Sex-specific programming,** such as higher reps or less rest for women.
   Reps-at-load barely differ by sex (Nuzzo 2024), and relative gains are
   similar (Roberts 2020 [VA]).
9. **Personal MEV, MAV or MRV presented as measured numbers.** No validated
   method exists, and the repo already bars this.
10. **An e1RM from high-rep sets (more than 10 reps) treated as a 1RM.**
    Accuracy falls as reps rise, and Reynolds 2006 advises 10 reps or fewer.
    Formulas also run low on the deadlift (LeSuer 1997 [VA]; by how much is
    unchecked [U]).
11. **Daily maxes or weekly max-effort days for consumers.** They come from
    elite or equipped contexts, with no evidence for novices.
12. **Prilepin's chart as a volume rule.** It comes from Olympic lifts and was
    never validated.
13. **Lengthened partials replacing full range of motion by default.** The
    evidence is early and mostly in untrained people. Choosing exercises that
    load the muscle at long length is the safe step.
14. **"Running kills gains."** On average there is no interference with
    hypertrophy or maximal strength (Schumann 2022: SMD −0.01 and −0.06). The
    costs are explosive strength, worst within one session (Schumann 2022),
    and lower-body strength in men (Huiberts 2024: −0.43 in men, 0.08 in
    women). Petré 2021's blunting in trained people was confined to
    same-session training.
15. **Velocity-based training as a default.** It needs hardware, and the
    benefits depend on the velocity-loss threshold chosen (Hickmott 2022).
16. **Very short rests (30–60 seconds) for "metabolic stress".** Longer rests
    grow at least as much (Schoenfeld 2016; Singer 2024).
17. **Outcome promises** ("+10 kg in 16 weeks", "bench bodyweight by
    summer"). Response heterogeneity is large (Hubal 2005; Ahtiainen 2016).
    Give ranges.
18. **Cutting training volume because the person is on a cut.** No checked
    source supports it. Helms 2015 prescribes a normal hypertrophy dose during
    contest prep (40–70 reps per muscle per session), but whether to keep
    pre-diet volume through a cut is [U]: its full text could not be read.
    Lift4 already retired this.
19. **Readiness questionnaires or HRV adjusting loads automatically.** Not
    researched here, and the support is weak.
20. **A per-person fitness–fatigue fit as a predictor.** Its parameters are
    ill-conditioned: individual fits give wide, highly correlated estimates
    (Hellard 2006: τa–τf r = 0.99), and predictive accuracy on new data was
    not tested. Use it for population simulation dynamics only.

## 8. Open questions, and what to verify before coding

**Unknown in the literature:**

- individual volume landmarks;
- whether any deload cadence beats autoregulated deloads;
- long-term (over 12 months) comparisons of progression models;
- how intermediates and advanced lifters, women and older adults respond
  beyond 16 weeks;
- whether app algorithms beat good fixed programmes;
- how accurately consumers rate reps in reserve over months;
- the frequency effect on strength once volume is controlled. Pelland 2025's
  model finds one (strength gain relative to 2 sessions a week: 0.73 at 1,
  1.14 at 3, 1.22 at 4), but Grgic 2018 found no significant volume-equated
  effect, so even its direction rests on one analysis;
- whether strength volume should be counted per muscle, per exercise or per
  movement pattern. Rhea 2003 counted sets per muscle group per session,
  Ralston 2017 per exercise per week, and ACSM 2026 suggests four to six body
  regions. No trial compares the rules;
- adherence to a lifting app from install, not from week 4. Conti 2026
  leaves out users who did not train every week of their first 28 days.

**Verify first.** Ordered by how load-bearing each is for the model. Where an
item was checked on 2026-10-07, it says what the check found and what is still
open.

1. **Pelland 2025.** Read in full as the SportRxiv preprint (v2) with the
   authors' OSF tables [VF]; the published text is closed.
   - Counting: fractional (0.5) counting fit best for both hypertrophy and
     strength, and direct-only counting fit weekly strength worst. The
     exploratory best weight for an indirect set is about 0.38–0.39.
   - Strength threshold: about 3–4 fractional sets a week (the point of
     undetectable superiority is 3). No direct-set threshold is reported.
   - Hypertrophy: a square-root curve in fractional sets, about 0.24% more
     gain per extra set at 12 sets a week.
   - Frequency at equal volume: strength 3.27% per extra session, nothing
     detectable past 3; hypertrophy 0.32% per session, compatible with none.
   - Not in the paper: any per-lift frequency figure.
2. **Nuzzo 2024.** Read in full [VF]. The main, bench and leg-press tables
   (means and SDs) come from its OSF files. Main lifts: 4.9 reps at 90%, 9.8
   at 80%, 14.8 at 70%. The SD falls from 4.4 reps at 60% to 1.7 at 95%.
3. **Latella 2020 and Steele 2023.** Latella 2020 [VF] gives rates, not a
   curve shape: men gained 0.15 kg a day on the total (about 10.7% of baseline
   a year, as the authors extrapolate), over a mean follow-up of 1.6–1.8
   years inside a 15-year window. The rate runs from first to best total, so
   it is biased upwards. Steele 2023 [VF] gives the shape: about 30–50% in
   year 1 and 50–60% by about 6 years, flat after 1–2 years (§4.1).
4. **Roberts 2020, Refalo 2025 and Jones 2021.** Effect sizes for sex
   differences, checked against the abstracts [VA] (§2.17). Relative growth
   is similar by sex. Women gain more relative upper-body strength (Roberts)
   and, over 50, more relative lower-body strength (Jones). Men gain more in
   absolute terms (Refalo; Jones).
5. **Wilson 2012, Schumann 2022, Huiberts 2024 and Petré 2021.** None gives
   numbers by running km. Interference is keyed to session separation (Petré
   2021: trained people were blunted only within one session), sex (Huiberts
   2024), and endurance frequency and duration (Wilson 2012, not confirmed by
   Schumann 2022). Wilson 2012's full text, which may hold thresholds, could
   not be read [U].
6. **Bickel 2011, Psilander 2019 and Bosquet 2013.**
   - Bickel 2011 [VF]: one ninth of the dose kept 1RM for 32 weeks, and
     stopping entirely lost 7% while staying 23% above baseline. Fibre size
     held in the young at both doses, and at neither dose in 60–75-year-olds.
   - Psilander 2019 [VF]: after 20 weeks off, size was back to baseline and
     about 60% of the strength gain was kept. Retraining was no faster than
     the first training.
   - Bosquet 2013 [U]: the full text is closed, and the abstract gives no
     rate by duration.
7. **Remmert 2025.** Read in full as SportRxiv preprint 537 [VF]. It had no
   journal version in PubMed, Europe PMC or Crossref on 2026-10-07; check again
   before coding.
8. **Busso 1990.** The weightlifting fitness–fatigue parameters [U]. The
   abstract confirms individual fits for six elite weightlifters over a year
   but gives no values, and the full text could not be opened. A secondary
   collection (Peng 2023) lists strength-sport fatigue time constants of
   13–22 days; that is not checked against the paper.
9. **App help pages.** Read for JuggernautAI, RP, MacroFactor Workouts,
   Fitbod, Alpha Progression and Gravl, plus Boostcamp, Liftosaur and Hevy
   (§5.1). Still open: Strong, and Alpha Progression's per-muscle volume
   targets in the app itself.
10. **Recent papers.** Enes 2024 and 2025 and Räntilä & Ahtiainen 2026 are
    read (§2.22). Still open: Conti 2026's supplementary hazard tables (S3,
    S11), if the simulator fits its dropout model to them.
