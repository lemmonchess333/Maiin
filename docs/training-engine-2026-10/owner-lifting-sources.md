## Principles (paraphrased)

**OREB-01 — Order of priorities.** Technique first; then how hard (effort/load);
then how much (volume); then how often (frequency); then the long-term
structure. Staying injury-free sits beside technique (BP 5:10). Nutrition
modifies everything: roughly 2 g protein per kg, maintenance or a small surplus,
no deficit while strength is the goal; beginners can still gain in a deficit,
advanced lifters much less so. (SQ 20:36–28:48, 39:25; BP 44:10)

**OREB-02 — Periodize effort, not just load.** Inside every 4-week phase the
session-average effort climbs: week 1 ≈ RPE 7 (about 3 reps to spare), week 2
≈ RPE 8, week 3 ≈ RPE 9, week 4 as close to the limit as possible. Start each
phase conservatively, like an even-paced marathon, so every session can
progress and the person learns the whole week's demands before pushing. He
calls RPE an imprecise estimate and accepts that. (SQ 25:02; BP 19:08)

**OREB-03 — Deloads are earned.** Year 1: no planned deload weeks; the
conservative week 1 of each phase is the built-in deload. After about a year of
serious training (heavier loads plus squat/deadlift systemic fatigue): on
week 4, keep pushing the heaviest loads but halve the sets (4 × 5 → 2 × 5),
because accumulated volume drives fatigue more than load does; then restart
at week 1. (BP 21:44, 34:31)

**OREB-04 — The long cycle.** A 16-week macrocycle of four 4-week phases that
moves from higher to lower reps (e.g. 10s → 8s → 5s → peak, or 8–10 → 6–8 →
4–6 → peak with 4–8-week phases), then repeats. Test a max (or compete) about
2–3 times a year; a test or meet is fatiguing and does not itself make anyone
stronger. (SQ 34:50; BP 16:14)

**OREB-05 — Maxing is a skill.** Rep strength does not convert cleanly to a
1RM: rep-max calculators mislead (his 200 kg × 10 did not mean the formula's
1RM), and the feel of a heavy unrack, walkout and brace has to be practised.
Peak with HEAVY SINGLES — submaximal singles with reps in reserve, not max
tests — introduced over ~4 weeks, heavier each week, followed by back-off
work. The reverse also holds: a heavy phase costs high-rep capacity. "You get
good at what you practise." (SQ 10:05–20:00, 34:50)

**OREB-06 — Failure: right exercise, right time.** Rarely or never train
barbell compounds to failure: strength is a skill and grinding reps is poor
practice. Failure is useful on machines, dumbbells and higher-rep hypertrophy
work. By stage: year 1 no failure (to refine technique — not because beginners
are lifting enough to get hurt); year 2 occasionally at or near failure in
week 4, always with a spotter or safeties; advanced lifters rarely. His biggest
early mistake: failure and forced reps every session, no progress, sore front
shoulders. (SQ 26:24; BP 17:56, 38:35–42:34)

**OREB-07 — Volume for strength.** About 8–12 hard sets per movement pattern
per week suits most lifters; ~6 can be enough for very demanding lifts or
elite lifters. Per session on a big lift, 4–6 working sets (advanced ≈ 4).
Hypertrophy: the commonly cited 10–20 sets per muscle per week. He attributes
the strength range to "Dr. Rhea and colleagues, 2014" (in OREB-27, "around
2009"). (SQ 28:48)

Checked 2026-10-07: PubMed lists no Rhea dose-response paper from 2009 or
2014 [VA]. The range matches the Arizona State meta-analyses:

- Rhea et al. 2003, _MSSE_ 35(3):456–64,
  doi:10.1249/01.MSS.0000053727.63505.D4 [VF]. 140 studies, 1,433 effect
  sizes. The largest mean gains came from 4 sets per muscle group: 3 days a
  week at 60% of 1RM for the untrained, 2 days at 80% for the trained (at
  least a year of training).
- Peterson, Rhea & Alvar 2004, _JSCR_ 18(2):377–82, doi:10.1519/R-12842.1
  [VF]. 37 studies of athletes: 8 sets per muscle group, 2 days a week, 85% of
  1RM.
- Peterson, Rhea & Alvar 2005, _JSCR_ 19(4):950–8, doi:10.1519/R-16874.1
  [VF]: a review that applies both (177 studies, 1,803 effect sizes).

They counted sets per muscle group per workout, not per week [VF]. 4 sets ×
2–3 days gives 8–12 a week, which is his range. Athletes: 8 × 2 = 16 [C].

Three cautions:

- The unit is the muscle group, not the movement pattern.
- The optimum is where pooled, uncontrolled pre–post effect sizes peaked. The
  data are thin at the top [VF]. In trained lifters the effect size was 0.47
  at 1 set, 1.0 at 3, 1.17 at 4 (from only 12 effect sizes) and 1.15 at 5.
- Later work flattens the curve. 2–3 sets per exercise beat 1, and 4–6 were no
  better (Krieger 2009, _JSCR_ 23(6):1890–901,
  doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181b370be [VA]; possibly his "around 2009"). Ralston
  2017 counted per exercise per week: 5–9 or ≥10 sets beat ≤5 only by a
  trivial margin (ES 0.15 and 0.18; doi:10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7 [VF]).
  ACSM 2026 says 2–3 sets a session at ≥80% of 1RM, at least twice a week
  (doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000003897 [VF]).

Verdict: 6–12 sets per pattern a week is a sound working range, not a
measured optimum. The per-pattern unit is his own. The nearest published
equivalent is ACSM 2026's four to six regions: upper and lower body, push and
pull, with upper-body push and pull optionally split into horizontal and
vertical [VF].

**OREB-08 — Frequency and heavy days.** Squat about twice a week for most
people. Pressing 3 times a week while building strength (not all bench: one
flat competition bench, one incline barbell, one overhead barbell), falling to
2 a week as strength rises. Never more than one HEAVY session per lift per
week: heavy/light at 2×, heavy/moderate/light at 3×. Space the sessions
(Mon/Wed/Fri). High-frequency specialization (squatting daily) can move an
intermediate faster but is a big commitment, is never required, and is
tolerated less with age and strength. (SQ 30:04–34:20; BP 9:34, 34:31, 43:04)

**OREB-09 — Variation first, specificity later.** "The pyramid only peaks as
high as the base is wide" (Louie Simmons): build a broad base early with
planned variation of the main lifts (angles, grips, barbell vs dumbbell), then
narrow toward the tested lift as the peak approaches. Keep at least one session
a week of the competition lift all year. (BP 6:17–9:30, 33:19)

**OREB-10 — Test on the strongest variant, build with the others.** Low-bar
squat and the competition bench setup (arch, legal grip, pause) express
strength best; high-bar and heel-elevated squats, a closer-grip full-range
incline and a full-range overhead press build it. The front squat is optional
for squat strength and leg size. He widened his bench grip one finger per phase
as the stretched pec got stronger. (SQ 22:07–24:30; BP 1:51–5:00, 26:40–28:15)

**OREB-11 — Pair push with pull.** One pull per press (1:1), supersetted to fit
twice the work in the same time; accessories cover the angles the main lift
neglects (a neutral-grip dumbbell press after a pronated barbell press). He says
outright that structural balance is practice, not research. (BP 5:10–8:10,
46:15, 48:46)

**OREB-12 — Priorities compete.** Train roughly 50/50 upper and lower unless
specializing; the more lifts you push at once, the harder top-end progress on
any one becomes; cut the other work as a peak nears. (BP 10:44–12:30)

**OREB-13 — Bodyweight sets the expectation.** Absolute strength rises with
bodyweight; bodyweight multiples favour lighter lifters. His claims for COACHED
lifters: a double-bodyweight squat within ~1–2 years of starting; a 100 kg bench
within the first year; 140 kg in about 2 years for men of 80–100 kg (a 70 kg
lifter benching 140 kg is 2× bodyweight, which he calls elite). Each further
plate takes longer; responders vary. (SQ 0:01–6:07, 21:04, 39:25; BP 0:36–1:40,
13:28, 43:53)

**OREB-14 — Safety on hard bench sets.** A spotter or safeties; no collars when
benching alone without safeties. (BP 41:28–42:30)

**OREB-15 — Hypertrophy execution.** On a hypertrophy accessory, chase the
target muscle's stretch and contraction to near failure rather than a strict
joint lockout; if in doubt, use the full range. (BP 23:17–26:30)

### OREB-VOL — The Debate: High Volume vs. Low Volume

Locators: 0:01 the debate · 1:08 the "junk volume" myth · 3:15 bodybuilders
are strong · 4:20 monthly undulating periodization (MUP) · 9:55 the 16-week
breakdown · 10:50 accumulation (10s) and hypertrophy technique · 12:11
intensification (4s) and strength technique · 15:15 the 8s and 2s · 17:59
accessories and exercise redundancy · 21:47 supinated pulldowns (Dorian Yates).

**OREB-16 — "Intensity" means % of max, not effort.** Above ~80% of 1RM is
heavy (strength work); below it a set can still be very hard. Keep load and
effort as separate dials. (VOL 0:27)

**OREB-17 — Against "only the last five reps count".** He rejects the
effective-reps argument for training mostly in sets of 5 or fewer: heavy
low-rep work every session beats up joints and invites injury in strong
lifters; high-rep phases build work capacity and muscle; the most muscular
people (bodybuilders) mostly train 8–20 reps. He frames this as practice
weighed alongside research. (VOL 1:08–4:10)

**OREB-18 — Muscle is strength potential.** Bigger cross-section, more
potential; bodybuilders are strong but unpractised at low reps and convert
quickly when given strength phases; the best strength athletes include
hypertrophy blocks. Six reps is his "sweet spot" for strength with
hypertrophy carry-over. (VOL 7:16–8:25)

**OREB-19 — Monthly undulating periodization (from Charles Poliquin).** His
critique of long linear phases: a long high-rep stretch detrains the strength
(neural) gains of the last heavy block, and a long heavy stretch loses the
work capacity and muscle of the last volume block. MUP alternates every
4 weeks: accumulation 10s → intensification 4s → accumulation 8s →
intensification 2s (16 weeks), then a 4-week peak if competing (≈20 weeks:
high, low, high, low, peak). Each accumulation steps down 2 reps, so does each
intensification, and the second accumulation runs heavier than the first.
NOTE: OREB-04 (linear 10s → 8s → 5s → peak) is ALSO his practice; he presents
both as valid. (VOL 4:20–17:00)

**OREB-20 — Pair a hypertrophy variant with the competition lift by phase.**
Accumulation: high-bar squat, mid-grip flat-foot bench with less arch and a
little more elbow flare (more pec), Romanian / stiff-leg / snatch-grip
deadlift. Intensification: low-bar squat, the competition bench, the
conventional (or sumo) deadlift. A strength athlete's hypertrophy is
"specific" — press-heavy, not flies and pec deck. (VOL 10:50–14:30)

**OREB-21 — Top set plus back-off sets.** Feel one heavy set, then back off to
accumulate volume; repeating heavy top sets is too demanding. A favourite way
to peak and to learn to handle heavy weights. (VOL 5:28–6:40)

**OREB-22 — Accessories follow their own rules.** The phase's rep target
applies to the MAIN lifts only — no 2-rep curls, leg extensions or calf
raises; pick the rep range that suits the exercise (calves barely respond to
heavy fives). Accessories cover the joint angles and ranges the main lift
missed and avoid "exercise redundancy" (no flat dumbbell press straight after
bench; a seated or incline press instead). Superset push with pull, triceps
with biceps. (VOL 17:59–21:45)

**OREB-23 — Keep accessories stable; vary the handle.** The same lat pulldown
52 weeks a year, changing only the grip (supinated / neutral / pronated)
month to month for elbow health. Small variation, not wholesale exercise
changes. (VOL 21:47–24:10)

### OREB-SPL — Training splits (mentorship lecture)

Locators: 0:00 why push/pull/legs may waste time · 1:46 availability first ·
4:03 1× vs 2× a week · 6:33 how many sets · 11:52 skill vs muscle · 14:50
strength volume · 19:06 2-day · 21:01 3-day (Lillibridge) · 25:37 PPL and
quads · 29:21 his 3-day · 32:13 4-day · 34:44 lower-body emphasis · 35:48
5-day (Poliquin) · 38:13 bro split · 47:39 6-day.

**OREB-24 — Availability decides the split.** Ask first how many days the
person can really train. Two days is a legitimate plan (busy people; field and
combat athletes whose sport must come first); 4–5 is his optimum for maximal
strength and size; 6 only for people who need the daily structure (he cites
mental health), with the same weekly volume spread thinner. (SPL 1:46–4:00,
47:39)

**OREB-25 — Frequency and effort trade off.** The more often you train, the
less hard each session should be; the less often, the harder. A 2-day plan is a
sprint: take sets close to failure, there are five days to recover. (SPL
4:26–5:05, 17:50–19:05)

**OREB-26 — Two sessions per muscle, mainly for set quality.** With volume
equated, research shows little difference between 1, 2 or 3 sessions per
muscle (and studies are short, often ~8 weeks — "a guide, not a leader"), but
splitting the week's sets over two sessions keeps late sets out of the fatigue
that makes them "junk volume". Two a week is his default; three adds little.
(SPL 4:03–11:15)

**OREB-27 — Start volume low, add it when progress stalls.** Hypertrophy:
10–20 hard sets per muscle per week (warm-ups don't count) — start near 10 and
add sets (12, 14 …) as the lever when a muscle plateaus. Strength: 6–12 sets per
MOVEMENT PATTERN per week (he dates the Rhea citation "around 2009"; see
OREB-07 for the papers). Volume load matters: the stronger the lifter, the
longer the rest between sets and between sessions for the same sets × reps. In
practice he builds a session (main lift, a complementary second lift, a
low-cost third for arms or joint health — rotator cuff, serratus, core, hips)
to fit about an hour rather than counting sets; main-lift top sets run 3–5 for
a beginner, 1–2 for a very strong lifter. (SPL 6:33–9:46, 13:00–16:00)

**OREB-28 — Diminishing returns are still returns.** The first set of an
exercise returns the most, each extra set less; elite lifters accept small
returns, beginners need not chase them. (SPL 38:53–40:17)

**OREB-29 — His split recommendations.** 2 days: whole body both days (push,
pull, squat, hinge), varied between days (hip- vs knee-dominant squat
pattern; vertical vs horizontal push), compounds first, not upper/lower.
3 days: whole body ×3 works for most; the Lillibridge layout (squat + deadlift
day alternating heavy/light, heavy bench + balanced upper day, whole-body
day); his own — lower/squat-quad day, whole upper day, whole body led by the
deadlift plus a second quad exposure (at least leg extensions). Push/pull/legs
×1 gives presses and quads one session a week — not recommended. DUP (squat,
bench, deadlift every session at heavy/moderate/light) is great for skill but
too much for very strong lifters and needs added upper-back work. 4 days (his
"gold standard"): upper (horizontal) / lower (quad) / upper (vertical) / lower
(posterior, plus quad accessories). 5 days: squat 2×, upper 2×, deadlift 1×
(his refinement of Poliquin's rotating lower ×3 / upper ×2). Bro split: works,
but 1× frequency, late-session junk volume and 4 upper days to 1 lower. (SPL
19:06–47:30)

**OREB-30 — Absolute strength sets recovery.** Recovery demand scales with
the absolute load, not the bodyweight multiple: a 60 kg woman pulling 4×
bodyweight (240 kg) can train 5 days; a 200 kg strongman pulling 400 kg+
deadlifts heavy every second week. Heavy spinal loading more than once a week
is rarely tolerated by very strong lifters (hence squats and deadlifts on one
day in Lillibridge); squats recover faster than deadlifts. (SPL 23:40–24:40,
27:40–28:40, 32:30–33:50, 44:38–47:35)

**OREB-31 — Emphasis is a preference, not a sex rule.** Many of his female
clients ask for more lower body and less upper-body muscularity; he offers a
lower-emphasis variant (one full upper day, a full-body day in place of the
second) while stressing that women need not train differently. (SPL
31:06–32:05, 34:44–35:45)

**OREB-32 — Recovery tiers.** Sleep and nutrition first; sauna, cold and
massage are optional extras. (SPL 37:45–38:10)

### OREB-SEL — Exercise selection and exercise order (mentorship lecture)

Locators: 1:45 why selection and order make or break a programme · 6:19
exercise redundancy · ~8:15 ancillary (low-cost) exercises · 14:53 the
exception for elite lifters · 19:27 case study, Björnsson's deadlift · 22:38
the 8-category model · 26:08 are sissy squats or guillotine presses bad ·
32:07 template 1, a 4-week hypertrophy block · 41:25 template 2, a strength peak.

**OREB-33 — Order follows the goal.** The first exercise is the one most
relevant to the person's goal: usually the most specific, complex,
stimulating and fatiguing. People get best at what they do first (group A
doing A-B-C-D ends best at A; group B doing D-C-B-A ends best at D). Difficulty
falls through the session. (SEL 1:45–4:30, 7:28–7:50)

**OREB-34 — Avoid exercise redundancy.** After the main lift, don't pick "the
second-best exercise for that muscle"; pick one that covers joint angles,
ranges of motion or muscles the main lift neglected, and that is less
fatiguing. Two exercises that train the same muscle through the same joint
actions with the same resistance profile (flat barbell press then flat
dumbbell press) add fatigue, not stimulus. There can be more than one
secondary when gaps remain. (SEL 1:45–3:00, 6:19–7:30, 13:30–14:20)

**OREB-35 — End with low-cost work.** The last slot is "ancillary": low
fatigue, usually isolation — joint health (rotator cuff, core, hips) or
"feel-good" pump work (glute pump, curls, calves, lateral raises) because
enjoyment raises effort and adherence (he credits Bret Contreras on training
morale). Never a deadlift as a finisher. Warm-up drills are not training: band
external rotations or a core warm-up done without progression or effort do not
count as training those muscles. (SEL 7:51–13:30)

**OREB-36 — The elite exception: top set + variation.** For very strong
lifters near a competition, specificity rules but enough volume of the
competition lift would exceed recovery. So: one heavy top set (often a single)
of the competition lift, then back-off volume on a close but LESS LOADABLE
variant — paused or 1¼ squat, paused Spoto press, deficit or snatch-grip
deficit deadlift, even flat dumbbell press — trained hard but at a much lower
absolute load, so less systemic fatigue. This is deliberate "redundancy".
Distinct from top set + back-off sets of the SAME lift, where the back-offs'
effort is reduced. (SEL 14:53–19:20)

**OREB-37 — Very strong deadlifters go heavy every second week.** Heavy and
light deadlift weeks alternate; the light week keeps the skill (a competition
lift is trained at least weekly). Björnsson's 16-week deadlift build has only
8 heavy exposures: a heavy single plus 2 × 5 snatch-grip deficit deadlifts at
roughly 60% of his competition load. (SEL 15:40–21:00)

**OREB-38 — The 8-category model.** He spreads effort and volume roughly
evenly over eight movement categories — the upper four are horizontal and
vertical push and pull; the lower ones include a KNEE-dominant squat pattern
(maximal knee bend, upright torso, knees forward — heel-elevated high-bar or
front squat, hack squat, sissy squat, knee-forward split squat) and a
HIP-dominant squat pattern (forward lean, vertical shin — low-bar squat,
forward-leaning Bulgarian split squat); lunges sit inside the squat
categories. (The transcript does not name all eight; do not invent the other
two.) Joint-health work, arms, neck and calves sit outside the model. A
low-bar squat followed by a leg press or Bulgarian split squat is redundant
(all hip-dominant); follow it with a knee-dominant choice. (SEL 6:43–7:05,
22:38–25:50)

**OREB-39 — Load profile decides the rep range.** Compound lifts that spread
load over many joints (low-bar squat, conventional deadlift, competition
bench) suit heavy low-rep work; targeted, lengthened-position exercises
(heel-elevated or knees-over-toes squats, sissy squats, the flared-elbow
"guillotine" press) suit lighter loads and higher reps and never 1RMs. Most
"exercise X is dangerous" claims ignore that context. Bench pec tears happen
near the chest, where the pec is most stretched and weakest, often as the
elbows flare — hence the heavy-bench cues to tuck the elbows and keep the chest
up. (SEL 26:08–31:40)

**OREB-40 — Gaps compound lifts leave.** Rectus femoris crosses hip and knee,
so squats and lunges under-train it — add a leg extension. The short head of
biceps femoris does not cross the hip, so hinges miss it — add a hamstring curl
or Nordic. Glutes: a hinge loads them lengthened, a hip thrust shortened — "not
one or the other: one and the other". (SEL 36:09–38:00, 39:30–41:00)

**OREB-41 — Template: a 4-week hypertrophy block, upper/lower ×2.** Squat day:
heel-elevated high-bar squat 3 × 8 → Bulgarian split squat → leg extension →
Copenhagen plank. Press day (supersetted push/pull pairs): close-grip bench
with barbell row; high-incline dumbbell press with pulldown; lateral raise
with rope face pull. Hinge day: snatch-grip deficit deadlift → hip thrust →
hamstring curl. The second upper day mirrors the first with a vertical
emphasis. The days can start anywhere as long as upper and lower alternate —
"I don't think of the calendar week". (SEL 32:07–41:00)

**OREB-42 — Template: a strength peak.** Squat day: competition squat 1 × 1 →
paused competition squat 2 × 4 → leg curl. Bench day: competition bench 1 × 1 →
paused Spoto press → triceps extension. Deadlift day: competition deadlift →
paused deficit deadlift 2 × 4 → leg extension. Peaks last about 4 weeks
because a big volume cut held longer starts to detrain. He rates heavy singles
— the bench above all — the riskiest rep choice there is. (SEL 41:25–45:00,
42:29–42:55)

**OREB-43 — His year.** A periodized plan sets each phase's stimulus (volume →
strength → peak) without naming sets or exercises; the mesocycle (his: 4
weeks) is the actual programme. His year: three 16-week macrocycles of four
4-week blocks (48 weeks) plus a 4-week "fun phase" over Christmas. (SEL
32:07–33:40)

### OREB-ROT — Rotary periodization (filmed bench session; title not supplied)

No chapters in the supplied text; locators are timestamps.

**OREB-44 — Rotary periodization (from Charles Poliquin).** The main lift's
rep target changes EVERY WEEK in three-week waves, each wave starting one rep
lower: 10-8-6, 9-7-5, 8-6-4, 7-5-3, 6-4-2, 5-3-1 — 18 weeks ending at a heavy
single (shorter runs start later, e.g. the last 12 weeks from 8-6-4). Why: no
long stretch of heavy low-rep work (hard on joints for strong and older
lifters — he is 43), no long stretch without high-rep volume, and every week
"forces" more weight on the bar (his 160 kg × 10 → 170 × 8 → 180 × 6).
Accessories do NOT follow the scheme. The plan is a guide; changing it on the
fly is autoregulation. (ROT 6:03–14:20, 16:38–18:20)

**OREB-45 — About 4% per rep, up to ~6 reps.** His rule of thumb: 1RM 100%,
2RM ≈ 96%, 3RM ≈ 92%, 4RM ≈ 88%, 5RM ≈ 84%, 6RM ≈ 80%; it drifts by 7–8 reps
and is unreliable at 10+, where people differ widely. Dropping two reps ≈ +8%.
Round every jump to the plates a gym really has: 2.5 kg steps (1.25 kg a
side); fractional plates are rare. (ROT 19:05–25:09)

Against the research, checked 2026-10-07 (the full reps-at-%1RM tables are in
[lifting-evidence.md](lifting-evidence.md) §2.15):

- His table is clearly steeper than lab sets to failure. From 1 to 6 reps it
  drops 20 points of 1RM. Nuzzo et al. 2024's meta-regression (_Sports Med_
  54(2):303–321, doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7 [VF]) drops 12.6 points on its
  main table and 14.5 on the bench press; Epley drops 16.7 and Brzycki 13.9 [C].
- In the lab data a rep costs about 2.5% of 1RM from 2 to 6 reps, and about 2%
  from 6 to 10 [C]. Nuzzo's tables stop at 95% of 1RM, so the 2-rep loads
  behind the first figure assume a bridge to 1 rep at 100%.
- At 6 reps, Epley gives 83.3% and Brzycki 86.1% [C]. His 80% sits below
  both.
- At 80% of 1RM the average lifter reaches 8.8 reps on the bench press and 9.8
  on other lifts [VF]. So his 6RM load, used as a working load, leaves about
  3–4 reps in reserve on average [C]. That fits OREB-47.
- Dropping from 6 reps to 4 adds about 6% to the load in the lab data and in
  Epley (5.6–6.0%) [C]. His own table adds 10% (80% to 88% of 1RM, the 8
  points he calls +8%) [C].
- Of the curves compared, his sits closest to Marzagao 2026's curve fitted to
  app logs, at heavy barbell loads (arXiv:2603.17495, a preprint [VF]). At a
  100 kg load it gives 94.9, 91.1, 87.9, 85.1 and 82.5% of 1RM for 2 to 6 reps
  [C]. That curve was fitted for consistency across logged sets, not checked
  against tested maxima [VF].

**OREB-46 — Structure before intuition.** Beginners should follow a written
structure: "how you feel is a lie" — a bad-feeling day can go well and a
good-feeling day badly. Training by feel and reactive deloads
(autoregulation) come later, with experience. (ROT 13:59–15:10)

**OREB-47 — Reps in reserve on main lifts, failure on small isolations.**
Leave reps in the tank on the main lifts so the whole session and the next
week stay high quality and there is room to progress; accessories are lighter
and safer, so push them closer to failure; take arm isolation work to failure
at the end — it costs little. (ROT 27:11–29:09, 40:54–41:39)

**OREB-48 — Supersets only save time.** No hormonal or potentiation benefit.
Strong lifters should rest fully on the main lift (he takes ~8 minutes
between heavy bench sets; 3–4 minutes when he benched under ~120–140 kg) and
superset the accessories; most clients have about an hour, so pairing
non-overlapping push and pull work is the smart default. (ROT 31:15–34:30)

**OREB-49 — Biarticular arm muscles need isolation.** The long head of the
triceps and the biceps cross the shoulder, so presses and rows barely change
their length; a compound-only plan leaves them under-developed. Add triceps
extensions and curls if arm size matters to the person (less so for, say, a
boxer). (ROT 37:39–40:12)

**OREB-50 — Rows through the full range.** Let the shoulder blades protract
and the upper back round at the stretch; rows suit higher reps than 10.
(ROT 34:43–35:51)

**OREB-51 — Bodyweight is the nutrition gauge.** Weigh daily and hold weight
steady (maintenance) or a small surplus to expect the strength jumps; in a
deficit, lower the expectation. (ROT 19:59–22:20)

**OREB-52 — Labels don't matter.** His plans are linear in trend, undulating
week to week, and "conjugate" in training several qualities at once (low-rep
main lift, higher-rep accessories). (ROT 25:27–26:28)

Not adopted from this source: "last rep syndrome" (a psychology cue, not a
programming rule); the postpartum return anecdote (training around pregnancy
and surgery is clinical territory, outside what the app should advise).

## Generator checks these sources imply (for the generator audit and the simulation)

Each is a CHECK to measure on generated plans, not a rule to impose — a
failing check is a finding for the owner, not an automatic fix.

1. Redundancy: no session pairs two exercises of the same movement category
   AND the same resistance profile (flat barbell press + flat dumbbell press),
   except a deliberate strength-peak "top set + variation".
2. Order: within a session, the main lift comes first and difficulty falls
   (compounds before isolations; no hinge as a finisher).
3. Coverage: across a week, push : pull ≈ 1 : 1 and horizontal : vertical both
   present; a knee-dominant and a hip-dominant lower pattern both present;
   on Build muscle with ≥ 3 days, a knee extension and a knee flexion appear
   (rectus femoris, short head of biceps femoris).
4. Frequency: each main lift, and quads and presses specifically, at least 2×
   a week where days allow (the PPL ×1 failure mode); deadlift heavy ≤ 1×/week.
5. Volume: hard sets per muscle per week land in ~10–20 for hypertrophy goals
   and ~6–12 per movement pattern for strength goals, given the days and time;
   warm-ups excluded. The strength band and its per-pattern unit are his; the
   papers behind his citation give 8–12 sets per muscle group a week, 16 for
   athletes (OREB-07).
6. Two-day plans: whole body both days, the two days varied in pattern
   (vertical vs horizontal push; knee vs hip dominant), compounds prioritised.

## For the simulation

- Persona anchors (coach claims, upper-plausible for coached lifters, not
  population means): an 80–90 kg man reaching a 2× bodyweight squat in 1–2
  years; 100 kg bench inside year 1; 140 kg bench in ~2 years at 80–100 kg.
  Reconcile with the research bands before using them as pass/fail lines.
- The virtual lifter needs: rep-range specificity (1RM expression depends on
  recent low-rep exposure; e1RM from high-rep sets is a biased predictor);
  per-set effort (reps in reserve) feeding both stimulus and fatigue; bodyweight
  and sex scaling; an energy-balance modifier; a training-age decay of gain
  rate; a fatigue state that effort waves and lighter weeks dissipate; and, if
  any RPE-driven option is simulated, RIR misjudgment as a stable personal
  bias, not set-to-set noise. Halperin 2022 (_Sports Med_ 52(2):377–390,
  doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x [VA]): people under-predict the reps they
  have left by 0.95 on average, with a between-person SD of 1.45 reps;
  set-to-set noise is not quantified (see
  [lifting-evidence.md](lifting-evidence.md) §4.4).
- A head-to-head the owner will care about: Lift4's Get stronger as built vs an
  Oreb-style strength track (effort waves, rep phases, 16-week cycle with
  heavy singles and a test) — reported as RANGES across model variants, never
  as proof, because the response model encodes assumptions.

## Non-adoptions

- Daily or twice-daily squatting as any default (OREB-08: his own caveat).
- Competition technique coaching (arch, leg drive, grip width) beyond the
  app's form content; the app can name the setup, not coach it.
- 1:1 push/pull as injury PREVENTION (he says it is not research-backed);
  pairing for time efficiency stands on its own.
- His timelines as promises: coached, self-selected, mostly men of 80–100 kg.
